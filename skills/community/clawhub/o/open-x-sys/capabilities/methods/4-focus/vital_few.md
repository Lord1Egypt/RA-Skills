# Vital Few

> `vital_few(x)` · Stage 4 · Focus
> **Prompt**: "Which 20% of ___ drives 80% of the result?"
> **Also heard as**: "What actually matters here?" · "Where do most of the problems come from?" · "What can I stop doing?"
> **Reveals**: the small share of causes behind most of the effect.

## What it is for

Effects are rarely spread evenly over their causes. A few customers bring most of the revenue, a few defects cause most of the complaints, a few tasks produce most of the value. Vital Few finds that concentration with data, so that attention goes to the few causes that matter and stops being spent on the many that do not. It is the quantitative way to focus, and it is as much about what to stop as about what to do.

## What it reveals

- **The actual distribution**: causes sorted by their share of the effect, with the cumulative total.
- **The vital few**: the small group that accounts for most of the result.
- **The real ratio**, which may be 90/10, 70/30 or nearly flat.
- **What can be reduced or stopped** among the rest.
- **Whether the concentration is stable** over time.

## What a good use looks like

- It uses real data: the person's own records, or sourced figures. The 80/20 rule is a pattern that often appears. It is not a law, and it is never assumed.
- The unit of cause and the measure of effect are chosen deliberately and stated: customers by profit and not by revenue, defects by cost and not by count. A different choice gives a different few.
- The data is sorted and accumulated, and the result is shown as it is. If the distribution is flat, that is the finding.
- It checks stability: are the vital few the same ones as last year? A few that keeps changing calls for a different response.
- It looks at the rest before dismissing it. The many may be where future growth is, may carry a rare large risk, or may be what keeps the few (the small customers who make the product credible to the large ones).
- It turns the finding into a change of attention: what gets more, what gets less, what stops.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Quoting 80/20 with no data.**
- **Categories that create the concentration.** Grouping things until one group is large proves nothing.
- **The wrong measure**: ranking by what is easy to count, not by what matters.
- **Cutting the many** without asking what they support.
- **A single period**, taken as permanent.

## It works with

Vital Few is the quantitative companion to **Leverage**, and often follows **Flow** (which supplies the quantities) and **Size It**. It supports **Bottleneck** (which few causes produce most of the delay?) and **Best Practice** (delete and simplify, as in Musk's algorithm). **Review** uses it to find which few changes produced most of an improvement.

## Teach-back

"This was Vital Few: take the real numbers, sort the causes by how much of the result each one produces, and add them up. See how few it takes to reach most of the total. Then ask what the rest is for before you cut it."

## The thinking behind it

- **Vilfredo Pareto** observed in the 1890s that wealth was distributed very unevenly, a small share of people holding a large share of it.
- **Joseph Juran** generalized the observation to quality management, named it the Pareto principle, and described "the vital few and the trivial many"; he later preferred "the useful many", because the rest is not to be despised.

OpenXSys simplifies nothing in the principle, and insists on one condition: the distribution is measured, never assumed.
