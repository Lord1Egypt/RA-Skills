# Success to the successful

> A reinforcing pattern. Two activities compete for a limited resource; the one that does better early is given more, so it does better still.

## The structure

Two (or more) people, products, teams or projects draw on the same limited resource: budget, attention, talent, shelf space, citations. The resource is allocated according to recent results. A small early lead brings more of the resource, which enlarges the lead. The other is starved, performs worse, and receives less. Two reinforcing loops run in opposite directions from one allocation rule.

## How to recognize it

- A **shared, limited resource** and an allocation that **follows past results**.
- The initial difference between the two was small, or partly a matter of chance or timing.
- Later, the gap is large and is explained by merit: "the other one simply was not as good".
- The same pattern at a larger scale: the well-known get more credit for the same work, the large get better terms.

## Where it usually breaks

- **Lost diversity.** The starved alternative might have been the better one in the long run, or the right one for conditions that have since arrived. Concentration makes the whole fragile.
- **The winner meets its limit**, and nothing else has been kept alive.
- **Merit is misread.** Decisions are made on results that the allocation itself produced.
- **It is hard to see from inside**, because each allocation decision looks reasonable by itself.

## Where to push

- Allocate at least part of the resource by **potential** or by need, not only by past results.
- Set a goal for the whole that requires both to do well.
- Give the weaker one a protected period, with its own measure.
- Separate the resources, so the two no longer compete for the same pool.
- Ask of any "obvious winner" how much of its lead it was given.

## What to check before naming it

Find the shared resource and the allocation rule. Check how large the original difference was. Ask what the neglected one would have needed.

## Sources

- **Peter Senge**, *The Fifth Discipline* (1990): the "success to the successful" archetype.
- **Robert K. Merton**, "The Matthew Effect in Science" (1968): already-recognized scientists receive disproportionate credit for comparable contributions, so advantage accumulates.
