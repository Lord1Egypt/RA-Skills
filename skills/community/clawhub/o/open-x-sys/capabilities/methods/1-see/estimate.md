# Size It

> `estimate(x)` · Stage 1 · See
> **Prompt**: "Roughly how big is ___, really?"
> **Also heard as**: "Is this a big deal or a small one?" · "How many ___ are there?" · "What would that cost, order of magnitude?"
> **Reveals**: orders of magnitude, from first numbers to a defensible estimate.

## What it is for

A great deal of confusion disappears once the size of a thing is known to within a factor of ten. Is this market a million or a billion? Does this cost matter next to that one? Size It builds a defensible estimate from numbers that are known or can be bounded, when no figure is available or when a quoted figure needs checking. It gives every other method a sense of proportion.

## What it reveals

- **The magnitude**, as a range, not a point.
- **The factors** it is made of, each with where the number came from.
- **Which factor carries the uncertainty**, and therefore what would be worth finding out.
- **How it compares** with something familiar, so the number means something.

## What a good use looks like

- If the number can simply be looked up, it is looked up, with source and date. The estimate then serves as the check on it.
- The quantity is broken into factors that can each be known, looked up or bounded: population × share × frequency × price.
- Each factor carries its basis: a source, a measurement, or stated reasoning.
- It gives a range (low, likely, high) and thinks in factors of ten.
- It reaches the answer by **two independent paths** (for example from the top down and from the bottom up) and explains any disagreement between them.
- It says which single factor, if known better, would narrow the range most.
- The result is set beside a familiar quantity.

## Where it goes wrong

- **False precision.** "$4.37 billion" from four guessed factors.
- **One path only**, so there is nothing to catch an error.
- **Hidden assumptions** inside a factor that looked like a fact.
- **Units and time periods mixed**: per year against per month, stock against flow.
- **Estimating what should have been looked up**, or quoting a figure without checking that it is plausible.

## It works with

Size It supplies numbers to **Flow**, **First Principles** (what must it cost at minimum?), **Vital Few** and **Weigh**. Any method that makes a quantitative claim can call on it. It is also a direct answer to a request such as "how big is this market?"

## Teach-back

"This was Size It: break the unknown number into factors you can know or bound, multiply, give a range, and get there a second way to check. Then ask which factor you would most want to know better."

## The thinking behind it

- **Enrico Fermi** was known for order-of-magnitude estimates from very little data, and for posing such questions to students; problems of this kind are now called Fermi problems.

OpenXSys simplifies nothing here, and adds one habit: every factor carries its source, and the estimate is checked by a second path.
