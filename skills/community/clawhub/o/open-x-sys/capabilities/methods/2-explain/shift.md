# Shift

> `shift(x, as)` · Stage 2 · Explain
> **Prompt**: "Show me ___ through the eyes of ___."
> **Also heard as**: "How does this look to the customer?" · "What would a regulator say?" · "Why do they see it so differently?"
> **Reveals**: what each position sees, wants and cannot see.

## What it is for

There is no view of a system from nowhere. What counts as the system, its purpose and its problems depends on where one stands. Shift looks at the subject from several real positions, one at a time. It explains disagreements that are not about facts, exposes what the person's own position hides from them, and is the right first move before any decision that affects more than one party.

## What it reveals

For each position:

- **What it sees**: which parts of the system are visible from there, and how they are described.
- **What it wants**: what a good outcome is, from there.
- **What it fears or must avoid.**
- **What it cannot see**: what is invisible or unimportant from that position.

And across positions: where they truly conflict, where they only misunderstand each other, and what the whole looks like when the views are combined.

## What a good use looks like

- The positions really differ: the one who uses, the one who pays, the one who operates, the one who regulates, the competitor, the one affected but never consulted. Three views that agree are one view.
- Each view is given in that position's own terms and at its strongest, so that someone in that position would say "yes, that is how it looks".
- It rests on evidence of what people in that position actually say and do. Views are attributed to positions, not put into the mouths of named individuals.
- It includes at least one position the person would not have thought of or would prefer not to hear.
- It distinguishes real conflicts of interest from differences of information. They need different responses.
- It returns to the person's own question with something changed: an option that looks different, a party whose reaction was not expected, a blind spot named.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Caricature.** A position reduced to a stereotype nobody would recognize.
- **Only friendly positions.**
- **Averaging** the views into a statement that offends nobody and says nothing.
- **Invented statements** attributed to real people.
- **A tour with no return**: five perspectives listed and the person's question left where it was.

## It works with

Shift opens *Make a decision*, because options look different from each affected position. It pairs with **Incentives** (what each position is rewarded for) and feeds **What-if** and **And Then What?** (how each party will respond). The **Boundary** lens asks the related question of who was left out entirely. It is one way to "orient" in Boyd's loop.

## Teach-back

"This was Shift: stand somewhere else on purpose. For each position ask what it sees, what it wants and what it cannot see, in its own words. Then come back and look at your question again."

## The thinking behind it

- **Peter Checkland**, *Systems Thinking, Systems Practice* (1981): in human affairs, what "the system" is depends on the worldview from which it is described; different worldviews give different, equally coherent systems.
- **C. West Churchman**, *The Systems Approach* (1968): taking a systems view begins with seeing the world through someone else's eyes, and every view of the whole is partial.
- **Heinz von Foerster**, *Observing Systems* (1981): the observer is part of what is observed.

OpenXSys simplifies: soft systems methodology is a full process of inquiry with groups of people; Shift takes its central move.
