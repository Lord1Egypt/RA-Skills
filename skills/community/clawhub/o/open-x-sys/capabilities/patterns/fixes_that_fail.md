# Fixes that fail

> A fix relieves the problem quickly. After a delay, a side effect of the fix makes the problem return, often worse, and the same fix is applied again.

## The structure

A problem symptom prompts a fix. The fix works in the short term (a balancing loop). The fix also has an unintended consequence that, after a delay, feeds the original problem (a reinforcing loop). Because of the delay, nobody connects the consequence with the fix, and when the symptom returns the natural response is more of the same.

## How to recognize it

- The same problem **comes back after every fix**, typically stronger.
- The fix clearly works at first, which is why it is trusted.
- There is a **delay** between the fix and the return, long enough that the two are not linked.
- The amount of fixing needed keeps rising.
- Typical forms: borrowing to cover a shortfall, overtime to meet a deadline, discounts to meet a sales target, patches over a design flaw, cutting maintenance to save cost.

## Where it usually breaks

- **The side effect is invisible or belongs to someone else**, so it is never counted.
- **Short horizons.** The fix is judged at the moment of relief.
- **The person who applies the fix is rewarded for the relief** and gone before the return.
- **Stopping the fix hurts immediately**, so it continues.

## Where to push

- **Lengthen the time horizon** over which the fix is judged, and show the whole cycle on one timeline.
- **Find the cause** the symptom comes from and work on that (see `methods/1-see/xray.md`).
- **If the fix is unavoidable, deal with its side effect** at the same time.
- **Use the fix only to buy time** for the real solution, and say when it ends.

## What to check before naming it

Show the timeline: fix, relief, delay, return. Show the mechanism by which the fix feeds the problem. Without that mechanism it may simply be a recurring problem with an outside cause.

## Source
