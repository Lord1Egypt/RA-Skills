# Materials: Southwest Airlines' early model

> **A ready subject for the Playground: a famous company.**
> **Status of these materials**: written on 2026-11-16 from published sources, from memory of those sources, and **not yet verified against the primary texts**. Each item carries a confidence mark. Recent events in particular must be checked: the company changed several long-standing practices in 2024 and 2025.

Confidence marks: **[A]** well established and widely reported · **[B]** reported by a named source, worth checking · **[C]** approximate, recent or commonly cited without a firm source.

## Origins

- **[A]** Incorporated in Texas in 1967 as Air Southwest Co. by Rollin King and Herb Kelleher. Legal challenges from existing airlines delayed the start.
- **[A]** Began scheduled service on 18 June 1971 among Dallas (Love Field), Houston and San Antonio.
- **[A]** Because it initially flew only within Texas, it was not subject to federal fare and route regulation, which governed interstate airlines until deregulation in 1978.

## The practices it became known for

- **[A]** A single aircraft family, the Boeing 737.
- **[A]** Point-to-point routes between city pairs, where most large US airlines used hub-and-spoke networks.
- **[A]** Frequent flights on short routes, often from secondary airports such as Dallas Love Field, Houston Hobby and Chicago Midway.
- **[A]** Very short turnarounds at the gate. **[B]** Company histories trace the "ten-minute turn" to 1972, when the airline sold one of its four aircraft and kept the same schedule with three.
- **[A]** No assigned seats, no meals, no first class, for most of its history.
- **[A]** No interline baggage transfers or connections with other airlines, for most of its history.
- **[A]** Low fares on its routes, with the car and the bus named as competitors as well as other airlines.
- **[A]** A workforce that was largely unionized, with flexible job boundaries and profit sharing.
- **[A]** For many years tickets were sold directly and not through the travel-agent reservation systems used by other airlines.

## Results

- **[A]** The airline reported an annual profit every year from 1973 to 2019, 47 consecutive years, in an industry with frequent losses and bankruptcies.
- **[B]** It grew into one of the largest US airlines by domestic passengers carried.
- **[B]** In the 2000s it benefited from fuel hedges bought before oil prices rose.

## Attempts to copy it

- **[A]** Several large airlines launched low-cost units modelled on it, including Continental Lite, United's Shuttle and Ted, and Delta's Song. All were closed.
- **[A]** Other low-cost carriers, such as Ryanair in Europe, studied it and built on its approach.

## Strains and changes

- **[A]** In late December 2022, after a winter storm, the airline cancelled a very large number of flights over about a week, far more than other airlines, and took days to recover. **[B]** Reported figures are around 16,700 cancellations. Explanations at the time pointed to crew-scheduling systems and to the difficulty of repositioning crews and aircraft in a point-to-point network.
- **[B]** In December 2023 the US Department of Transportation announced a 140 million dollar penalty related to that disruption.
- **[C]** In 2024 an activist investor took a stake and pressed for changes. The airline announced it would move to assigned seating and offer seats with extra legroom.
- **[C]** In 2025 it began charging for checked bags on most fares, ending a long-standing policy. **Check the current state of all of these before use.**

## Numbers worth sizing for yourself

- If an aircraft flying short routes spends 15 minutes on the ground between flights instead of 45, how many more flights a day can it make, and what does that do to the cost per seat?
- What does a single aircraft type remove from the costs of training, spare parts and scheduling?
- How does a network of connected point-to-point flights recover from a disruption compared with a hub?

## Points where accounts differ

- How much of the performance came from the operating model, and how much from culture, leadership, fuel hedging or timing.
- Whether the 2022 disruption was caused by the model, by under-investment in systems, or by both.
- Whether the changes of 2024 and 2025 abandon the model or adapt it.

## Primary and standard sources

- Michael E. Porter, "What Is Strategy?", *Harvard Business Review* (November–December 1996), which uses the airline as an example of activities that fit together.
- Southwest Airlines annual reports and Form 10-K filings with the US Securities and Exchange Commission.
- US Department of Transportation, consent order and announcements concerning the December 2022 disruption (2023).
- Jody Hoffer Gittell, *The Southwest Airlines Way* (2003), on coordination among employee groups.
- Contemporary reporting on the 2024 and 2025 changes, to be read for the current state.
