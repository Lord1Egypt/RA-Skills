# Boyd's loop

> **Master route.** Observe → orient → decide → act, fast.
> **Run it with**: "Run Boyd's loop on ___." · "Things are moving fast with ___. What do I do right now?"
> **Fits**: a situation that changes while you act: a competitive move, a crisis, a launch, a negotiation, a fast-moving market or technology. It is for when waiting for a full picture means the picture will be out of date.
> **Source**: John Boyd, in briefings given from the 1970s to the 1990s. Boyd published very little; the loop is known from his briefing materials and from accounts by those who worked with him.

## The four steps, in their own order

| # | Step | What it asks | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Observe** | What is actually happening now, including what does not fit my expectation? | Acting on last month's picture |
| 2 | **Orient** | What does it mean? How must my picture of the situation change? | New facts forced into an old view |
| 3 | **Decide** | Which action do I choose, as a hypothesis about the situation? | Waiting for certainty |
| 4 | **Act** | Do it, and watch what the action reveals | Deciding without testing |

Then observe again. **Orientation is the centre of the loop.** It is where experience, habits, culture and new information are combined into a picture, and it shapes what is noticed, what is chosen and how it is done. "Fast" means changing one's picture and one's action more quickly than the situation changes. It does not mean hurrying through four boxes.

## As OpenXSys methods

| Step | Method | How it is used here |
|---|---|---|
| Observe | **Watch** | Look at the signals now: what has changed since the understanding was last checked, with dates |
| Orient | **X-ray** or **Shift** | Rebuild the picture: what structure produces what is observed; how the other parties see it |
| Decide | **Weigh** | Choose among options, with heavy weight on reversibility and on how quickly each would teach something |
| Act | **Build** | The smallest real action that tests the picture |

## What a good run looks like

- **Observe** uses current evidence and states its date. For subjects that move, it looks things up now.
- It looks for the observation that contradicts the existing picture. That observation is worth more than ten that confirm it.
- **Orient** says explicitly what changed in the picture, and what the person had assumed that no longer holds. If the person has a system, this is a rewrite of its understanding.
- **Decide** prefers actions that are reversible and informative: a move that can be undone and that will show something soon.
- **Act** is a concrete, small, real step, and the run states what result would confirm the picture and what would refute it.
- The loop closes: a time to observe again is set, and a watch condition is recorded.
- Speed never overrides the guardrails. An action that spends, publishes, contacts people or cannot be undone is still the person's to approve. The route makes the person faster by shortening the time to a good small decision, not by acting for them.

## Where OpenXSys simplifies

- Boyd's own diagram is not a simple circle. It has feedback from every stage back to observation, and paths by which orientation guides action directly without an explicit decision. This route presents the four-step form and keeps his main point, that orientation is central.
- The loop comes from thinking about air combat and military strategy, where the aim includes disrupting an opponent's picture. OpenXSys uses it for keeping one's own picture current in a changing situation, and not as a method for confusing other people.
- Because Boyd left briefings and few writings, accounts of the loop vary. Specific statements attributed to him are checked before being repeated.

## Next choices that usually fit

**What-if**, once there is time to look further ahead. **Shell's scenarios**, to prepare for several developments at once. **Review**, after the situation settles, to see which observations were missed.
