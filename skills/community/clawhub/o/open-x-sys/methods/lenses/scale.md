# Scale

> `scale(x)` · Advanced lens
> **Prompt**: "What changes about ___ at ten times the size, or a tenth?"
> **Use it when**: something that worked small is about to grow; something large is being copied small; a comparison is made across very different sizes; growth plans assume everything simply multiplies.

## What it shows

Things do not stay the same when they change size. Some quantities grow in proportion, some grow faster, some slower, and at certain sizes the thing has to be organized differently or it stops working. Scale asks which features of the subject depend on its size, and what it becomes at another one.

- **What grows in proportion**, and what does not: costs that fall per unit, coordination that rises faster than headcount, risks that appear only at volume.
- **Thresholds**: sizes at which a new structure becomes necessary (a second level of management, a formal process, a different technology).
- **Levels**: how the thing is built of parts within parts, and which behavior belongs to which level.
- **The right scale for the question**: the size at which the pattern the person cares about is visible.

## What a good use looks like

- It picks the quantities that matter and asks of each how it changes with size, with a mechanism and with evidence from cases at other sizes.
- It distinguishes what gets relatively cheaper with size from what gets relatively more expensive.
- It looks for the thresholds, because that is where plans fail: what works for five people, fifty, five hundred.
- It considers both directions. Shrinking exposes what was being carried by scale; growing exposes what was being carried by closeness.
- It keeps levels apart. A property of the whole is not a property of each part, and the reverse.
- Where it quotes a quantitative scaling relation, it gives the source and the domain in which it was measured.

## Where it goes wrong

- Multiplying everything by ten.
- A scaling law from one domain applied to another without evidence.
- Explaining behavior at one level with causes from a different level.

## It works with

Scale refines **Open** (at which size is this the thing we mean?), **Size It** (magnitudes), **What-if** (growth scenarios) and **Design** (what must change before growth). The **Moving bottleneck** pattern is often what a change of scale reveals.

## Teach-back

"This was Scale: imagine the thing ten times larger and ten times smaller. Ask what grows faster than size, what grows slower, and at what point it would have to be organized differently."

## The thinking behind it

- **Herbert A. Simon**, "The Architecture of Complexity" (1962): complex systems are hierarchies of nearly decomposable parts, with different dynamics at different levels.
- **Geoffrey West**, *Scale* (2017): many properties of organisms, cities and companies change with size in regular, non-proportional ways.
