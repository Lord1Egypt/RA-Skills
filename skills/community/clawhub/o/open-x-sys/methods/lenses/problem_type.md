# Problem Type

> `problem_type(x)` · Advanced lens
> **Prompt**: "What kind of problem is ___: clear, complicated, complex or chaotic?"
> **Use it when**: a method keeps failing on a problem; people argue about how to proceed; a best practice is being forced onto a situation it does not fit.

## What it shows

Problems differ in kind, and the right way to act depends on the kind. Treating one kind as another is a common and expensive error.

| Kind | Cause and effect | What works |
|---|---|---|
| **Clear** | Evident to everyone | Recognize the situation and apply the established practice |
| **Complicated** | Knowable, with analysis or expertise | Analyse, consult those who know, then choose among good practices |
| **Complex** | Visible only in hindsight; the system responds to being probed | Run several small safe-to-fail trials, see what emerges, amplify what works |
| **Chaotic** | No discernible relation in the moment | Act to stabilize first, then find out where you are |

There is also the state of **not knowing which kind it is**, in which people fall back on their habitual way of acting.

## What a good use looks like

- It classifies **parts** of the problem. Most real situations are mixed: a complicated core with a complex human response around it.
- It gives the evidence for the classification: has this been done reliably before? Do experts agree? Did past interventions have the effects that were predicted?
- It draws the consequence. The point of the lens is what it does to the method: analysis and a plan for the complicated part, small trials and watching for the complex part.
- It notices movement. Problems shift kind: a clear routine can collapse into chaos through complacency, and a complex situation becomes complicated as patterns stabilize.

## Where it goes wrong

- Used as a label, with nothing following from it.
- Everything declared "complex", as a way to avoid analysis that would in fact work.
- A complex problem handled with a detailed plan and a single large bet.

## It works with

It is a useful first check before **Best Practice** (established practice fits clear and complicated problems; complex ones need trials) and **What-if** (how far can anything be projected?). In complex situations, favor cheap first tests, **Watch** and frequent **Review**.

## Teach-back

"This was Problem Type: before choosing how to act, ask whether cause and effect here are evident, knowable with study, visible only afterwards, or absent. Plan what can be planned, and probe what cannot."

## The thinking behind it

- **David Snowden and Mary Boone**, "A Leader's Framework for Decision Making", *Harvard Business Review* (2007), presenting the Cynefin framework. The 2007 article names the first domain "simple"; later versions call it "obvious" and then "clear", the name used here.

OpenXSys simplifies: Cynefin is a sense-making framework used with groups; this lens uses its four ordered and unordered domains to choose a way of working.
