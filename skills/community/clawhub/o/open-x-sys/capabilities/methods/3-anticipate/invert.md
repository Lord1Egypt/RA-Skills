# Invert

> `invert(x, goal)` · Stage 3 · Anticipate · **starter method**
> **Prompt**: "What would make ___ fail?"
> **Also heard as**: "What could go wrong?" · "How would I ruin this?" · "It is a year from now and it failed. Why?"
> **Reveals**: the ways it could go wrong, and how to avoid them; a premortem.

## What it is for

It is hard to say what will make something succeed and much easier to say what will make it fail. Invert turns the question around: instead of asking how to reach the goal, it asks what would guarantee missing it, and then avoids those things. It finds risks that optimism hides, and it often produces a clearer plan than the forward question does, because avoiding the few fatal errors is most of what success requires.

## What it reveals

- **The ways it fails**, specific to this system and this goal.
- **Which are likely and which are fatal**, ranked.
- **Which are already under way.**
- **For each: how to avoid it**, or the early sign that it is starting.
- **What not to do**, which is frequently the most useful output.

## What a good use looks like

- The goal is stated first. Failure only has meaning against it.
- It uses the premortem framing: assume the goal was missed, at a definite future date, and write the history of how it happened. Stating the failure as a fact frees people to name causes they would otherwise not voice.
- The failure modes come from this system's actual structure: its bottleneck, its incentives, its loops, its dependencies. A generic risk register (competition, funding, execution) is not an inversion.
- Internal causes are included. Most failures are produced inside.
- They are ranked by likelihood and by damage, and the fatal few are separated from the survivable many.
- It checks which are already happening, with evidence.
- Each important one is turned into an avoidance, a safeguard, or a signal to watch.
- It is honest in both directions: it neither softens a likely failure nor inflates an unlikely one.

## Where it goes wrong

- **A list of generic risks** that would fit any project.
- **Fear without ranking**: thirty things that could go wrong, none more than another.
- **Only outside causes**: the market, the competition, bad luck.
- **Stopping at the list**, with nothing turned into an action or a signal.
- **Inverting to argue against the idea**, when the purpose is to make it survive.

## It works with

Invert follows **What-if** and **And Then What?** in *Make a decision* and precedes **Weigh**. It opens **Munger's checklist**. It is valuable just before **Design** and **Build** (how would this fail in use?). Its early signs become **Watch** conditions, and its ranked list feeds **Leverage**.

## Teach-back

"This was Invert: instead of asking how to succeed, ask what would guarantee failure, and be specific to your situation. Rank the answers, notice which have already started, and then do not do those things."

## The thinking behind it

- **Charlie Munger**, speech at the Harvard School, Los Angeles (1986): approaching a problem backward, by asking what would guarantee the outcome one wants to avoid. He credits the mathematician **Carl Jacobi** with the maxim to invert.
- **Gary Klein**, "Performing a Project Premortem", *Harvard Business Review* (2007): imagining that a project has already failed, and working out why, before it starts.

OpenXSys simplifies: it joins Munger's habit and Klein's exercise into one method and ties the failure modes to the structure found in earlier stages.
