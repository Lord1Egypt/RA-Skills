# Weigh

> `weigh(options)` · Stage 5 · Decide
> **Prompt**: "Should I ___ or ___?"
> **Also heard as**: "Which one?" · "Is it worth it?" · "Should we raise prices or cut costs?"
> **Produces**: options side by side: value, cost, risk, reversibility, what each closes off.

## What it is for

A decision is a choice among options under uncertainty, and most bad decisions come from comparing the options badly: on different terms, with one option's costs and another's benefits, or with far too much care for a choice that could be undone tomorrow. Weigh sets the real options side by side on the same dimensions, makes reversibility explicit, and gives a recommendation with the conditions under which it would change.

## What it produces

- **The real set of options**, which is usually wider than the two that were asked about.
- **A comparison on common dimensions**: value, cost, risk, reversibility, what each closes off, how soon each would show whether it was right.
- **What would have to be true** for each option to be the best one.
- **A recommendation**, with its confidence and what would change it.
- **The cheapest way to learn more** before committing, when the choice is hard to undo.

## What a good use looks like

- It questions the framing. "Raise prices or cut costs" may have a third option, a combination, a sequence, or "neither yet". Doing nothing is always an option and is weighed like the others.
- Every option is described on the same dimensions, each at its best, with the evidence for each cell. Unknowns are marked as unknown.
- **Reversibility is treated as the dimension that sets the level of care.** A choice that can be undone cheaply should be made quickly and tried. A choice that cannot be undone deserves slow, careful work. Most choices are of the first kind and are treated as the second.
- It counts what each option closes off: the opportunity given up, the path that becomes unavailable.
- It draws on the earlier stages: how each affected party sees each option (**Shift**), what follows from each (**What-if**, **And Then What?**), how each fails (**Invert**).
- It commits to a recommendation and says under which conditions another option would win.
- For a one-way choice it looks for a way to make it two-way: a pilot, a smaller version, a stage with an exit.
- The decision remains the person's. The page makes it easier to decide and does not decide for them.

## Where it goes wrong

- **A list of pros and cons** with no weights and no conclusion.
- **Numerical scores** that give false precision to judgments.
- **A false choice** accepted as given.
- **The same care for every decision**, slow where speed would teach more.
- **A hidden recommendation**, or none.
- **Comparing a detailed plan with a vague alternative.**

## It works with

Weigh ends *Make a decision*, after **Shift**, **What-if**, **And Then What?** and **Invert**, and ends **Munger's checklist**. It is the "decide" step of Boyd's loop. **Best Practice** answers "how" once Weigh has answered "which". The decision, its reasons and the signals that would reopen it are recorded in the person's system, so that **Review** can later compare intent with result.

## Teach-back

"This was Weigh: write down all the real options, including doing nothing. Compare them on the same terms. Then ask how hard each is to undo: if it is easy, choose fast and learn; if it is not, slow down and look for a smaller first step."

## The thinking behind it

- **Jeff Bezos**, 2015 letter to Amazon shareholders: some decisions are one-way doors, nearly irreversible, and should be made slowly and carefully; most are two-way doors, reversible, and should be made quickly by people with good judgment.
- **Opportunity cost**, a basic idea of economics: the cost of a choice includes the best alternative given up.

OpenXSys simplifies: Bezos wrote about how a large company should decide; the distinction is applied to any choice, with the added question of how to turn a one-way door into a two-way one.
