# What-if

> `whatif(x, if)` · Stage 3 · Anticipate
> **Prompt**: "What happens to ___ if ___?"
> **Also heard as**: "What if we double ___?" · "What happens when ___ runs out?" · "Where is this heading?"
> **Reveals**: consequences, delays and several plausible futures.

## What it is for

Once it is known how a system works, the natural next question is what it will do. What-if changes one condition and follows the change through the structure: through the flows, the loops and the constraints, with their delays. Its purpose is not to predict one future. It is to show the few futures that are really possible, what separates them, and what would announce each one early.

## What it reveals

- **The path of consequences** from the changed condition, step by step, with the mechanism at each step.
- **Delays**: when each effect arrives, which is often later than people expect.
- **Several plausible futures**, each resting on a stated difference in something uncertain.
- **Early signals** that would show which future is arriving.
- **What holds in all of them**, and can therefore be relied on.

## What a good use looks like

- It runs on an explicit understanding of the system. A what-if with no model behind it is a guess with a narrative.
- The change is specific: how much, where, starting when.
- Each consequence is derived through a named mechanism, and quantities appear wherever the understanding supports them.
- Other actors adapt. Competitors, customers and regulators respond, and the futures include their responses.
- The futures differ for structural reasons, tied to the one or two uncertainties that matter most. "Best, base and worst" with the same story at three volumes is not several futures.
- Each future has an early signal that can actually be observed.
- It states its confidence and what the model cannot tell, and falls back to a checked prediction where one is possible: a past change of the same kind, and what happened then.
- The person sees before and after side by side.

## Where it goes wrong

- **One forecast**, stated with confidence.
- **No delays**, so effects appear instantly and reversals are missed.
- **A static world**, where nobody else reacts.
- **Scenarios that differ only in adjectives.**
- **Extrapolating a trend** as if the structure that produced it will never change.

## It works with

What-if needs **Loops**, **Flow** or **Bottleneck** underneath it, and is followed by **And Then What?** (the next round of consequences), **Invert** (how it fails) and **Watch** (the early signals become watch conditions). It sits in *Make a decision* and is the centre of Shell's scenarios. When the person changes a condition in conversation ("and if the budget were half?"), this is the method being asked for.

## Teach-back

"This was What-if: change one thing, then follow it through how the system actually works, remembering that effects take time and other people react. Aim for a few futures with a reason for each, and a sign that would tell you which one is arriving."

## The thinking behind it

- **Jay W. Forrester**, *Industrial Dynamics* (1961): following a change through stocks, flows and feedback, and the finding that complex systems often respond in ways intuition does not expect.
- **Pierre Wack**, "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead" and "Scenarios: Shooting the Rapids", *Harvard Business Review* (1985): scenarios exist to change how decision-makers see their world, by preparing for several futures instead of predicting one.

OpenXSys simplifies: Forrester's method is quantitative simulation and Wack's is a long organizational practice; What-if reasons through structure, builds a simulation only when the dynamics need one, and says which it did.
