# And Then What?

> `second_order(x, action)` · Stage 3 · Anticipate
> **Prompt**: "If we ___, and then what?"
> **Also heard as**: "What are the side effects?" · "What happens after that?" · "What am I not thinking of?"
> **Reveals**: the consequences of the consequences.

## What it is for

The first effect of an action is usually the one that was intended, and everybody sees it. The effects that decide whether the action was wise come later: how others respond to the first effect, and what those responses cause. And Then What? follows an action at least two rounds past the obvious. It is the difference between a decision that looks right and one that turns out right.

## What it reveals

- **First-order effects**: the immediate, intended result.
- **Second-order effects**: how people and the system respond to that result.
- **Later rounds**, as far as the reasoning stays sound.
- **Effects that reverse the first**: the response that cancels or outweighs the gain.
- **Where the cost lands**, on whom and when.

## What a good use looks like

- Each round asks the same concrete question: who notices this, and what do they do about it? Customers, competitors, employees, suppliers, regulators, and the system's own feedback loops.
- It works through time: within days, within months, within years. Many actions are good at one horizon and bad at another.
- It looks specifically for the response that undoes the benefit: the discount that trains customers to wait, the hire that slows the team, the rule that is gamed.
- It keeps magnitudes in view. A second-order effect that is real and tiny should not outweigh a first-order effect that is real and large.
- Confidence drops with each round, and the page shows that it does. The first round may be near certain and the third a possibility worth watching.
- It asks what everyone else already expects. If the obvious consequence is already assumed by all, the advantage lies in seeing the next one.
- It ends with what this changes about the action: do it differently, do it together with something else, or watch for a named signal.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Stopping at the intended effect.**
- **Endless chains** of speculation presented with the same confidence throughout.
- **Only bad consequences**, or only good ones.
- **No actors.** Consequences that happen by themselves, with nobody responding.
- **Paralysis**: so many possible effects that no recommendation follows.

## It works with

And Then What? follows **What-if** in *Make a decision* and precedes **Invert** and **Weigh**. It draws on **Incentives** and **Shift** to know how each party will respond, and on **Loops** to know how the system will. It is the "think a level deeper" step of Munger's checklist. Signals it finds can become **Watch** conditions.

## Teach-back

"This was And Then What?: after the first effect, ask who notices and what they do. Then ask it again. Look especially for the response that cancels the gain, and trust each further round a little less."

## The thinking behind it

- **Howard Marks**, *The Most Important Thing* (2011): second-level thinking, which asks not only what will happen but what happens next, what others expect, and how one's own view differs from the consensus.

OpenXSys simplifies: Marks writes about investing, where the consensus is already in the price; the method applies the same discipline to any action in a system where others respond.
