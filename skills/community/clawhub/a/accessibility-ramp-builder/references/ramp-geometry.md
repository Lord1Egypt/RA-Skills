# Accessibility Ramp — Reference

## 1. The geometry that matters
A ramp is a ratio problem wearing a safety costume.

| User | Max ratio | Grade | Why |
|---|---|---|---|
| Self-propelled wheelchair | 1:12 | 8.3% | the user's arms are both engine and brakes |
| Assisted chair (pushed) | 1:10 | 10% | a second person adds braking, but not on ice |
| Walker / ambulatory | 1:8 | 12.5% | legs + hands, short distances |

**The hazard is descending, not climbing.** A hand-propelled chair on
a 1:8 grade accelerates faster than the user can react; braking with
gloved hands on railings pitches the chair. Front casters lift on the
transition, and a stop at the bottom pitches the occupant forward — or
the chair tips backward on a stall. 1:12 is where users with ordinary
upper-body strength stay in control.

Run length = `rise × ratio`:
- 22 in rise, self-propelled → 264 in = **22 ft** of run
- 30 in rise, walker → 240 in = 20 ft

People anchor on the rise ("it's only two steps!") and have no gut feel
for the 20+ ft of yard those steps cost. That mismatch is why the
hardware-store 2-ft ramps exist and why people fall off them.

## 2. Landings
- **Top and bottom: 60 in × 60 in, level.** The top landing is where
  the door is — a person sitting on a slope cannot pull a door open.
  If the door swings out over the ramp, the landing must contain the
  arc (or convert the door to swing in).
- **Rest platforms: every 30 in of rise** (ADA's maximum rise per
  run). A 30-in rise ramp needs one mid-platform; 60-in needs two.
- **Turning platforms: 60 × 60 in** at direction changes
  (switchbacks). A wheelchair needs a 60-in turning circle when the
  user is pushing.

## 3. Widths, rails, edges
- Clear width 36 in minimum (38–40 in comfortable for hands + rails
  on both sides; 48 in if the user is turned/passed).
- Handrails both sides whenever rise > 6 in: 34–38 in high, 12 in
  horizontal extensions top and bottom, continuous inner surface so
  a sliding hand never leaves the rail.
- Edge protection: 2 in curb or rail barrier so a caster drift can't
  drop off the side.
- Cross-slope ≤ 1:48 (~2%): sideways tilt is the unappreciated
  tip-over force on any turn; check with a level app in both axes.
- Surface: slip-resistant decking or grit strips; ¼-in gaps max so
  castors don't chatter; no gratings aligned with travel.

## 4. Doors — the classic $4,000 mistake
The ramp is the visible half; the door is the half people measure
wrong:
- clear opening ≥ 32 in (measure door open, frame to door face);
- below 32: **swing-clear (offset) hinges** buy ~2 in for ~$40 — the
  first fix, always; then wider frames, or removing the door;
- threshold ≤ ½ in with beveled edges, else add a threshold wedge to
  the ramp landing;
- lever handles, not knobs — wrist strength is part of "accessibility."

## 5. Layouts when the yard is short
- **Straight**: needs `run + 5 ft (landing)` along the entry wall.
- **L-shape**: turn at 90° with a 60×60 platform — footprint bends
  around a corner.
- **180° switchback**: two parallel runs + turn platform; wall-side
  footprint ≈ `run/2 + 5 ft`, depth ≈ two ramps side by side (7–8 ft).
- **Platform steps** (walker users who can stand a moment): 1:8
  max, much shorter footprint, rails both sides, each step 4–7 in.
- **Vertical platform lift (VPL)**: when geometry fails — $3–5k
  installed, needs power, maintenance, and a failsafe battery for
  outages.
- **Re-grading**: sometimes the cheapest ramp is removing the rise —
  a concrete berm or re-routed path eliminates the problem.

## 6. Materials & cost
| Build | Installed $/lf | Notes |
|---|---|---|
| Wood (treated lumber) | $100–250 | DIY-able (~low band in materials), frost footings, needs sealing/grip |
| Modular aluminum | $150–300 | removable/resale-able, no footings, fastest install |
| Concrete | $200–400 | permanent, zero maintenance, drainage must be designed in |

(Per-lf × total structure length including landings.)

## 7. Rules, permits, reality
- ADA is legally binding for public accommodations — for homes it's
  the reference standard, not a mandate; these numbers are the safety
  floor regardless.
- Attached structures usually need a permit; frost-depth footings in
  freeze climates; call-before-you-dig for post holes.
- Plan for the winter: ice film on any grade is un-walkable — site
  the ramp out of downspout splash, add grip, and budget de-icing
  (calcium chloride, pet-safe grades).
- Renting vs owning: modular aluminum rents for post-surgery
  recovery (~$200/mo) — the right answer for a 3-month need.
