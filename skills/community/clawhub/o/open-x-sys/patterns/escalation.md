# Escalation

> Two parties each measure themselves against the other. Each one's move threatens the other, who responds, and both spend more and more for no lasting relative gain.

## The structure

A and B each judge their position relative to the other. When A acts to get ahead, B sees a threat and acts to restore its position, which A sees as a threat. Two balancing loops, each party trying to close its own gap, combine into one reinforcing loop. Each side experiences its own moves as defensive.

## How to recognize it

- **Matching moves** over time: price cut answered by price cut, feature by feature, spending by spending, statement by statement.
- Each side explains its action as a response to the other.
- Both are **worse off in absolute terms** than when it began, while their relative position has barely changed.
- The contest has drifted away from what either party originally wanted.
- Typical forms: price wars, advertising races, arms races, bidding wars, feature races, feuds between departments, arguments.

## Where it usually breaks

- **Exhaustion.** One side runs out of resources, or both do.
- **A third party benefits** while the two are occupied.
- **It jumps to a new and costlier dimension** when the first is exhausted.
- **Neither can stop alone**, because stopping looks like losing.

## Where to push

- **Change what is measured.** Replace the relative goal (ahead of them) with an absolute one (good for our customers, sustainable for us).
- **De-escalate unilaterally and visibly**, with a small step that is safe to take, and see whether it is matched.
- **Talk.** Much escalation rests on each side misreading the other's defensive move as aggression.
- **Find a larger goal** that both need and neither can reach while fighting.
- **Compete on a different dimension**, where the other's moves are not a threat.
- **Decide in advance where to stop.**

## What to check before naming it

Show the sequence of matched moves with dates. Show that each side's goal is relative. If only one side is responding, it is not escalation.

## Source

- **Peter Senge**, *The Fifth Discipline* (1990): the "escalation" archetype.

Related ideas appear in the study of arms races and in game theory. They are not needed to recognize the pattern, and specific attributions are given only when checked. The **Shift** method shows how each side sees the other's moves. **Invert** asks how one would make it worse.
