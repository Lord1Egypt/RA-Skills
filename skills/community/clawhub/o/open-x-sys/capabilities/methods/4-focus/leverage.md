# Leverage

> `leverage(x, goal)` · Stage 4 · Focus · **starter method**
> **Prompt**: "Where would a small change in ___ matter most? My goal is ___."
> **Also heard as**: "Where should I put my effort?" · "What is the one thing to change?" · "What would actually move this?"
> **Reveals**: the few places worth pushing, ranked by effect and cost.

## What it is for

In any system most interventions change little and a few change a great deal. Leverage finds the few. It converts an understanding of how something works into an answer to "where should effort go?", and it is the hinge between seeing clearly and deciding what to do. Without it, understanding produces a long list of improvements, and the person is no better placed to choose.

## What it reveals

- **A short ranked list** of places to intervene, with the expected effect and the cost of each.
- **The depth of each**: a number, a feedback path, an information flow, a rule, a goal, a belief.
- **The direction to push**, which is not always the intuitive one.
- **What resists**, and what side effects to expect.
- **What is not worth doing**, named as such.

## What a good use looks like

- The goal is explicit. Leverage is always relative to a goal, and a different goal has different leverage points.
- Candidates come from the structure already found: the loops, the bottleneck, the incentives, the flows, the conventions that first principles showed to be optional. Leverage that does not rest on an understanding is a guess.
- Each candidate states its depth. Adjusting a number (a price, a budget, a headcount) is the most common intervention and usually the weakest. Changing who has what information, what is rewarded, what the rules are or what the system is for usually goes further and is harder.
- The direction of the push is checked. People often find the right place and push the wrong way.
- It says what will push back: the balancing loop, the party whose incentive is touched, the delay before results appear.
- The ranking shows effect against cost, in the person's real currencies, with confidence.
- It is short: two or three, perhaps one. The rest are listed briefly as not worth the effort now, with the reason.
- The top candidate comes with a cheap way to test it.

## Where it goes wrong

- **A list of improvements.** Fifteen good ideas is the absence of leverage.
- **A parameter change presented as leverage.**
- **No goal.**
- **The right place, the wrong direction.**
- **Ignoring resistance and delay**, so the intervention is abandoned just before it works.
- **Deep change recommended lightly.** Changing a system's goal is powerful, slow and costly, and the person should hear that.

## It works with

Leverage follows **Loops**, **Bottleneck**, **Incentives** and **First Principles**, and leads to **Best Practice** (how to act at that point) and **Weigh**. It sits in *Solve a recurring problem* and *Open my system*. **Vital Few** is its quantitative companion. **Invert** checks the chosen point before effort is spent.

## Teach-back

"This was Leverage: state the goal, then look in the structure you found for the few places where a push travels furthest. Prefer changing a feedback, a rule or what people can see over changing a number. Check which way to push, and expect something to push back."

## The thinking behind it

- **Donella Meadows**, "Leverage Points: Places to Intervene in a System" (1999): an ordered list of places to intervene, from parameters, which are weakest, up through feedback loops, information flows, rules and goals to the paradigm out of which the system arises; with the observation that people often push leverage points in the wrong direction.

OpenXSys simplifies: Meadows gives twelve places and warns that the order is tentative; the method uses the order as a guide to depth and ranks candidates for this system by effect and cost.
