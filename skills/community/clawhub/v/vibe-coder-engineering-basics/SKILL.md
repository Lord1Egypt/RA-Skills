# Vibe Coder Engineering Basics

## Headline

Learn 107 Software Engineering Basics in Plain Words, Then Check What Your App Needs

## Hook

Give your AI agent one app. It teaches you the engineering basics behind it, in plain words, then checks which ones your app needs now, which ones later, and which ones not yet, with a fix draft for owner review.

## Pain, in plain words

Many apps are built fast with an AI agent, and they work on day one. Then a password leaks, a backup does not exist, a slow page drives users away, or a small change breaks the whole app. The builder never learned the basics, because no one explained them in simple words. This skill teaches the basics first, in plain words, then checks what your app actually needs, so you fix the right things first.

## What you get

A plain words lesson for 107 software engineering basics, grouped by theme: security, auth and identity, scaling and performance, reliability, and shipping and teamwork. For each term, what it is, when an app needs it, and what happens if it is missing. Then a need check for your app: Now, Later, or Not yet, with evidence, and a fix draft for owner review, never a changed live app by itself.

## Who this is for

People who build apps with an AI agent, and want to understand what is behind the app, in simple words. People who run a small business app, and want to know what is missing, and what to fix first. People who are not technical, and want to ask their builder the right questions, with the right words.

## Why this saves money

A taught owner stops paying for fixes they do not need yet, like scaling for 5,000 users when the app has 40. A need check stops a missing backup, or a leaked secret, from turning into a lost app, or lost customers. A fix draft, reviewed first, stops a live app from changing in a way the owner did not approve.

## Technical, for coders

Structured teach and check guide for 107 software engineering basics, grouped by theme, ready to brief any AI agent, or a human builder. Each term has a one line plain words explanation, and core terms in each group have a deeper teach and check: what it is, when an app needs it, what happens if it is missing, and how to check it with pasted evidence. No guarantee of more users, more sales, or a higher rank, and no claim that learning a term alone makes an app safe, fast, or ready. The fix draft is for owner review, not a live change.

## Purpose

Teach 107 software engineering basics in plain words, then check which ones one app needs now, later, or not yet, with a fix draft for owner review. The skill teaches first, then checks, then drafts. It does not change a live app, publish anything, or change live code, without a separate approval from the owner.

## How to use it

Fast path: If the user already gives the app details up front, app type, what it does, how many users, how people log in, where data lives, and what is slow or broken, do NOT ask a long list of questions first. Teach the terms that fit, run the need check, and give the fix draft in the SAME reply, then list what is still Not confirmed. Never stop at questions alone.

Hard rule, read first: Your AI agent teaches from the 107 terms in this skill only. It never invents a term, a definition, or a status. It checks only evidence the user pastes in the message. It never marks a need as confirmed without pasted evidence. If the pasted evidence says nothing about a term, mark it Not confirmed, and say what is needed to check it. A term is never marked Now, Later, or Not yet on a guess.

Step 1: ask for the app details, or use the details already pasted, and list which terms fit this app.
Step 2: teach the terms that fit, one line each first, then the deeper teach for the core terms in each group.
Step 3: run the need check, Now, Later, or Not yet, with evidence quoted from the pasted details.
Step 4: write the fix draft, labelled DRAFT, NOT SENT, with owner approval needed.
Step 5: run the missing checks at the end, and list what is complete, missing, or not confirmed.

Ask only what is missing, in plain words, one short list at a time:
1. What does the app do, in plain words, and who uses it?
2. How many users does it have now, and how many do you expect in a year?
3. How do people log in, and who can see whose data?
4. Where does the data live, and is there a backup?
5. What is slow, broken, or worrying you most right now?
6. What does your app already do well, that must not change?

If the user does not know an answer, mark it Not confirmed, and say what is needed to check it. Do not invent an answer. If the user says use the live app for a question, use only what is visible on that app, and say so.

## The 107 terms, in plain words

Every term below has a one line plain words explanation. The numbers are the source list numbers, kept so the owner can find a term again. Read the whole list first, then teach only the terms that fit the app in front of you.

### Group A: Security, 26 terms

3. Environment Variables: settings kept outside the code, like keys and addresses, so they can change without rewriting the app.
4. Secrets Management: a safe place to store keys and passwords, so they never sit in the code where anyone can read them.
27. HTTPS / TLS: the lock that scrambles data between the visitor and the app, so no one in the middle can read it.
30. SQL Injection: an attack where a visitor types database commands into a form and the app runs them by mistake.
31. XSS (Cross-Site Scripting): an attack where a visitor puts a harmful script on a page and it runs in another visitor's browser.
32. CSRF: an attack where a bad site tricks a logged in visitor's browser into doing something on your app without them knowing.
33. Dependency Vulnerabilities: known holes in the ready made code your app uses, found by checking the list of libraries.
40. File Upload Security: rules that stop a visitor uploading a harmful file and running it on your server.
74. Data Privacy (GDPR): rules about collecting, storing, and deleting people's personal data, and telling them what you do with it.
81. Content Security Policy: a rule list that tells the browser which sites it may load scripts and images from, and blocks the rest.
82. Security Headers: short instructions the server sends with every page, telling the browser how to stay safe.
83. Clickjacking: an attack where a bad page hides your page inside it, so a visitor clicks something they cannot see.
84. SSRF: an attack where a visitor makes your server call a private address it should never reach.
85. Open Redirects: a flaw where a link on your site can send a visitor to a bad site instead of the right one.
86. Path Traversal: an attack where a visitor changes a file path in a request to read files they should never see.
87. Command Injection: an attack where a visitor types system commands into a field and the server runs them.
88. Mass Assignment: a flaw where a visitor can change fields they should not touch, like their own role, by adding them to a request.
89. IDOR: a flaw where a visitor changes an ID in a link and sees another person's data.
100. Cookie Flags (HttpOnly, Secure, SameSite): settings on cookies that stop scripts reading them, stop them going over plain HTTP, and stop other sites sending them.
101. Secret Rotation: changing keys and passwords on a schedule, so a leaked old key stops working.
102. Audit Logs: a record of who did what and when, so you can trace a problem back to its cause.
103. Encryption at Rest: scrambling stored data, so a stolen hard drive or database backup is unreadable.
104. Encryption in Transit: scrambling data while it moves between the visitor, the app, and the database.
105. Hashing vs Encryption: hashing turns data one way and cannot be undone, encryption can be undone with the right key.
106. Salting: adding a random value to each password before hashing it, so two people with the same password get different results.
107. Penetration Testing (likely, not confirmed from the screenshot): a friendly test where someone tries to break into your app on purpose, to find the holes before a real attacker does.

### Group B: Auth and identity, 15 terms

5. Authentication: proving who you are, usually with a password, a code, or a passkey.
6. Authorization: what you are allowed to do after you prove who you are.
28. Session Management: keeping a visitor logged in safely, and ending the session when they log out or it expires.
29. Two-Factor Authentication: a second check after the password, usually a code on the phone, so a stolen password alone is not enough.
34. Principle of Least Privilege: give each person and each part of the app only the access it truly needs, nothing more.
90. Brute Force Protection: slowing down or blocking repeated password guesses, so an attacker cannot try thousands in a row.
91. Account Lockout: locking an account for a while after too many wrong password tries.
92. Password Reset Flows: the safe way to let a user set a new password, with a link that expires and works only once.
93. Magic Links: a login link sent by email that logs you in with one click, no password needed.
94. Passkeys: a login that uses your phone or computer to prove who you are, with no password to steal.
95. Single Sign-On (SSO): one login that opens many apps, so a person signs in once and is known everywhere.
96. Role-Based Access Control: giving people a role, like admin or viewer, and letting the role decide what they can do.
97. Row-Level Security: database rules that let each user see only their own rows, never another person's rows.
98. API Keys: a secret code that lets one app call another app, so the other app knows who is calling.
99. Token Expiration and Refresh: login tokens that expire after a short time, with a safe way to get a new one without asking for the password again.

### Group C: Scaling and performance, 19 terms

1. Load Balancing: spreading visitors across more than one server, so no single server gets all the work.
2. Rate Limiting: setting a limit on how many requests one visitor can make, so one person cannot flood the app.
11. Database Indexing: a lookup list for the database, like an index in a book, so it finds rows fast instead of reading everything.
12. Database Migrations: small, tracked steps that change the database shape, so the change can be tested and undone.
14. Caching: keeping a copy of a slow answer, so the next visitor gets it fast without asking the database again.
15. CDNs: copies of your files kept on servers near your visitors, so pages and images load fast everywhere.
36. Connection Pooling: reusing a small set of database connections instead of opening a new one for every visitor.
37. Database Normalization: organizing tables so each fact is stored once, so updates do not leave old copies behind.
44. Cache Invalidation: clearing an old cached copy when the real data changes, so visitors never see stale answers.
45. Read Replicas: copies of the database that only answer read requests, so the main database is free to write.
46. Horizontal vs Vertical Scaling: horizontal means adding more servers, vertical means making one server bigger.
47. Stateless Services: servers that keep no memory of a visitor between requests, so any server can answer any visitor.
64. Load Testing: sending many fake visitors at once to see where the app slows down or breaks, before real visitors do.
66. Profiling: measuring which part of the code is slow, so you fix the real bottleneck, not a guess.
69. Concurrency: many things happening at the same time, and keeping them from stepping on each other.
35. Race Conditions: when two things change the same data at the same time, and the result depends on which one finishes first, so the answer can be wrong.
70. Async / Await: a way to start a slow job, like a payment, and let the app do other work while it waits for the answer.
72. Cost Monitoring: watching what the app costs to run, so a busy day or a bug does not quietly burn money.
77. Performance Budgets: a limit you set, like pages must load in under 2 seconds, and a check that stops you shipping slower pages.

### Group D: Reliability, 24 terms

7. Input Validation: checking what a visitor types before the app trusts it, and rejecting anything that does not fit.
8. HTTP Status Codes: short numbers the server sends back, like 200 for OK, 404 for not found, and 500 for a server error.
9. Error Handling: what the app does when something goes wrong, so the visitor sees a clear message and nothing breaks silently.
10. Automated Testing: tests the computer runs by itself, so a change that breaks something is caught before it goes live.
13. Backups: copies of your data kept somewhere safe, so you can bring the app back after a failure or a mistake.
16. Logging: writing down what the app did, so you can find out what happened when something goes wrong.
17. Monitoring: watching the app all the time, so you know it is up and healthy, not only when a user complains.
18. Timeouts: a limit on how long the app waits for an answer, so a slow service cannot freeze the whole app.
19. Exponential Backoff: waiting longer and longer between retries, so a failing service is not hammered while it recovers.
20. Idempotency: making a request safe to repeat, so a double click or a retry never charges or creates the same thing twice.
21. Circuit Breakers: a switch that stops calling a failing service for a while, so the failure does not spread to your whole app.
38. Soft Deletes: marking a row as deleted instead of removing it, so it can be brought back if the delete was a mistake.
39. Data Validation on the Server: checking data again on the server, never trusting only the check in the browser.
48. Health Checks: a small check the app answers, so the server knows if the app is alive and ready for visitors.
49. Graceful Shutdown: letting the app finish what it is doing before it stops, so no visitor's work is cut off halfway.
50. Alerting: a message to your phone when something breaks, so you hear about it fast, not from a user hours later.
51. Distributed Tracing: following one visitor's request across every service it touches, so you can see where it got slow.
52. Structured Logging: writing logs in a fixed format, so a computer can search and count them, not only a human reading lines.
53. Error Tracking: collecting every error in one place, with how often it happens and who it hit, so you fix the worst first.
54. Uptime Monitoring: a check every minute that the app answers, so you know the moment it goes down.
55. Feature Flags: a switch that turns a new feature on or off without shipping new code, so a bad feature can be turned off fast.
56. Staging Environments: a copy of the app where you test changes first, so a mistake never reaches real users.
71. Memory Leaks: memory the app takes and never gives back, so it slowly gets slower until it crashes.
73. Disaster Recovery: a tested plan to bring the app back after a big failure, like a lost database or a dead server.

### Group E: Shipping and teamwork, 23 terms

22. Reverse Proxies: a front door server that takes visitor requests and passes them to the right app server behind it.
23. API Gateways: one front door for all your services, that checks who is calling, limits requests, and sends each call to the right service.
24. Docker: a box that holds your app and everything it needs, so it runs the same on any computer.
25. CI/CD: a pipeline that tests and ships your code automatically when you push a change, so shipping is boring and safe.
26. Message Queues: a waiting line for jobs, so a busy app can take requests now and do the slow work later, one by one.
41. Webhooks: a message your app sends to another app the moment something happens, like a payment, instead of waiting to be asked.
42. Background Jobs: slow work done away from the visitor's screen, like sending emails, so the page does not wait for it.
43. Cron Jobs: jobs that run on a schedule, like every night at 2am, without anyone starting them by hand.
57. Infrastructure as Code: writing your servers and settings as files, so you can rebuild them the same way every time.
58. Code Review: another person reading a change before it ships, to catch mistakes and share knowledge.
59. Branching Strategies: rules for how people work on separate copies of the code and merge them back without breaking each other.
60. Semantic Versioning: version numbers that mean something, major for breaking changes, minor for new features, patch for fixes.
61. API Versioning: keeping old versions of your API working while you ship a new one, so old apps do not break overnight.
62. API Documentation: clear written instructions for how to call your API, what it needs, and what it returns.
63. Unit vs Integration vs End-to-End Tests: unit tests check one small piece, integration tests check pieces working together, end to end tests check the whole app like a real user.
65. Debugging: finding why something is wrong, step by step, with evidence, instead of guessing and changing things at random.
67. Time Zones: handling dates and times carefully, storing one standard time and showing each user their own local time.
68. Character Encoding: making sure text in any language, with any symbol, is stored and shown correctly.
75. Accessibility: building the app so people with different abilities, like screen reader users, can use it fully.
76. Mobile Responsiveness: making the app work well on a small phone screen, not only on a big computer screen.
78. Technical Debt: shortcuts taken to ship fast that must be paid back later, or they slow every future change.
79. Reading Documentation: finding the answer in the official guide first, before guessing or asking for help.
80. Reading Error Messages: reading the actual error message carefully, because it usually says exactly what went wrong.

## Teach deeper, core terms in each group

Use the one line above first. Then, for the core terms below only, teach deeper in this order: what it is, when your app needs it, what happens if it is missing, how to check it. Teach only the core terms that fit the app in front of you. Never teach a term as if it fits when the pasted evidence says nothing about it.

### Group A core terms

Secrets Management. What it is: keys and passwords live in a safe store, not in the code, and the app reads them when it starts. When your app needs it: the moment the app uses any key, like a database password, a payment key, or an email key. What happens if it is missing: a key in the code can leak when the code is shared, copied, or posted, and anyone with the key can use your services. How to check it: ask the owner to paste where the keys live now, code, a settings file, or a safe store, and quote that as evidence. Never ask the owner to paste a real key.

SQL Injection. What it is: the app builds a database question from what the visitor typed, without checking it first, so a visitor can type a command instead of a name. When your app needs it: any app with a search box, a login, or a form that talks to a database. What happens if it is missing: a visitor can read, change, or delete data they should never see. How to check it: ask the owner to paste how the app asks the database, or whether the builder uses safe queries, and quote that as evidence.

HTTPS / TLS. What it is: the lock in the browser address bar, scrambling everything between the visitor and the app. When your app needs it: every app, from day one, even a small one, because logins and forms travel over the internet. What happens if it is missing: anyone on the same network can read passwords and data as they travel. How to check it: ask the owner to paste the app address, and whether the browser shows the lock, and quote that as evidence.

Hashing vs Encryption. What it is: hashing turns a password one way, and even the owner cannot turn it back, while encryption scrambles data so it can be unscrambled later with the right key. When your app needs it: any app that stores passwords must hash them, and any app that stores private data must encrypt it. What happens if it is missing: a stolen database gives away every password in plain words. How to check it: ask the owner to paste how passwords are stored, hashed or plain, and quote that as evidence.

### Group B core terms

Authentication. What it is: the app checks who you are before it lets you in, with a password, a code, or a passkey. When your app needs it: any app with accounts, private data, or paid features. What happens if it is missing: anyone can open the app and act as anyone else. How to check it: ask the owner to paste how people log in, and quote that as evidence.

Authorization. What it is: after login, the app checks what this person may do, and blocks the rest. When your app needs it: any app with more than one kind of user, like admin and customer, or any app where one user must never see another user's data. What happens if it is missing: a normal user can open admin pages, or see another customer's orders. How to check it: ask the owner to paste what each role can do, and whether a normal user was ever able to open an admin page, and quote that as evidence.

Two-Factor Authentication. What it is: after the password, the app asks for a second proof, usually a code on the phone. When your app needs it: any app with money, private data, or admin accounts. What happens if it is missing: a stolen password alone is enough to get in. How to check it: ask the owner to paste whether a second step exists on login, and quote that as evidence.

Row-Level Security. What it is: database rules that let each user see only their own rows, so even a bug in the app cannot show another person's data. When your app needs it: any app where users have their own data, like orders, bookings, or messages. What happens if it is missing: one wrong link can show another user's private data. How to check it: ask the owner to paste whether the database has rules per user, and quote that as evidence.

### Group C core terms

Caching. What it is: the app keeps a copy of a slow answer, so the next visitor gets it fast. When your app needs it: any page or list that is slow, or asked for again and again. What happens if it is missing: every visitor waits for the database, and the app gets slow as users grow. How to check it: ask the owner to paste which page is slow, and how long it takes to load, and quote that as evidence.

Load Balancing. What it is: visitors are spread across more than one server, so no single server carries all the work. When your app needs it: when one server is too busy, or when the app must stay up even if one server fails. What happens if it is missing: one busy day can bring the whole app down. How to check it: ask the owner to paste how many servers the app runs on, and quote that as evidence. With one server and few users, mark it Not yet, not Now.

Database Indexing. What it is: a lookup list for the database, so it finds a row fast instead of reading every row. When your app needs it: any list or search that is slow, especially as the data grows. What happens if it is missing: searches get slower every week as more rows are added. How to check it: ask the owner to paste which search is slow, and how many rows the table holds, and quote that as evidence.

Rate Limiting. What it is: a limit on how many requests one visitor can make in a minute, so one person cannot flood the app. When your app needs it: any app with a login, a form, or a public page that anyone can hit again and again. What happens if it is missing: one attacker can slow the app for everyone, or guess thousands of passwords. How to check it: ask the owner to paste whether a limit exists on login or on forms, and quote that as evidence.

### Group D core terms

Backups. What it is: copies of your data, kept somewhere safe, and tested by restoring them. When your app needs it: every app, from day one, because data loss has no undo button. What happens if it is missing: one mistake, one bug, or one lost server can delete everything, with no way back. How to check it: ask the owner to paste when the last backup ran, where it is kept, and whether a restore was ever tested, and quote that as evidence. A backup that was never restored is Not confirmed, not confirmed.

Monitoring. What it is: watching the app all the time, so you know it is up, fast, and healthy, without waiting for a user to complain. When your app needs it: any app with real users, even a small one. What happens if it is missing: the app can be down for hours, and the owner hears about it last. How to check it: ask the owner to paste what watches the app, and who gets the alert, and quote that as evidence.

Automated Testing. What it is: tests the computer runs by itself after every change, so a change that breaks something is caught before it goes live. When your app needs it: any app that changes often, or any app where a broken login or payment would hurt. What happens if it is missing: every small change risks breaking something, and the break is found by a user, not by a test. How to check it: ask the owner to paste whether tests exist, and what they cover, and quote that as evidence.

Idempotency. What it is: making a request safe to repeat, so a double click, or a retry after a bad connection, never charges or creates the same thing twice. When your app needs it: any app that takes payments, creates bookings, or sends orders. What happens if it is missing: one double click can charge a customer twice, or create two bookings. How to check it: ask the owner to paste what happens when the same payment or booking is sent twice, and quote that as evidence.

### Group E core terms

CI/CD. What it is: a pipeline that tests your code and ships it automatically when you push a change, the same way every time. When your app needs it: any app that ships changes more than once in a while, or any app with more than one builder. What happens if it is missing: every release is done by hand, so every release can go wrong in a new way. How to check it: ask the owner to paste how a change goes live now, by hand or by pipeline, and quote that as evidence.

Docker. What it is: a box that holds your app and everything it needs, so it runs the same on any computer. When your app needs it: any app that must run the same on a builder's computer, a test server, and a live server. What happens if it is missing: it works on one computer and breaks on another, and no one knows why. How to check it: ask the owner to paste whether the app runs in a box, or straight on the server, and quote that as evidence.

Staging Environments. What it is: a copy of the app where changes are tested first, with test data, before they reach real users. When your app needs it: any app with real users, so a mistake is found in staging, not in front of a customer. What happens if it is missing: every change is tested live, on real users, with real data. How to check it: ask the owner to paste whether a test copy exists, and where changes are tested, and quote that as evidence.

Semantic Versioning. What it is: version numbers that mean something, major for breaking changes, minor for new features, patch for small fixes. When your app needs it: any app or API that other people use, so they know when an update will break their work. What happens if it is missing: an update breaks other apps without warning, and no one can tell which version is safe. How to check it: ask the owner to paste the current version number, and how updates are numbered, and quote that as evidence.

## Need check

Run the need check only on terms that fit the app, and only on pasted evidence. Show each term like this:
- Term: [term name, with its source number]
- Need: [Now, Later, or Not yet, or Not confirmed]
- Evidence: [the pasted text quoted as evidence, or Not confirmed]
- Why: [one line, in plain words]

Use Now when the app needs it today, and the pasted evidence shows it is missing. Use Later when the app will need it as it grows, and the pasted evidence shows it is not urgent today. Use Not yet when the app does not need it at its current size, and the pasted evidence shows why. Use Not confirmed when the pasted evidence says nothing about the term. Never mark Now, Later, or Not yet without pasted evidence. Never mark a term confirmed on a guess.

## Hard rules

- Teach from the 107 terms in this skill only. Never invent a term, a definition, or a status.
- Never mark a need as Now, Later, or Not yet without evidence pasted in the message.
- Never ask the owner to paste a real password, a real key, or real customer data. Ask where it lives, or how it is stored, in plain words.
- Never change a live app, publish anything, or change live code, without a separate approval from the owner.
- Never use a real business as an example. Examples in this skill are fictional, labelled Example only.
- Suggested items must be marked Suggested, not confirmed.
- A fix draft is not a promise of more users, more sales, or a higher rank.
- Keep the draft in plain, short sentences. The owner may not be a technical reader.
- Item 107, Penetration Testing, is likely, not confirmed from the screenshot. Say so every time it is taught, and never state it as confirmed.
