# Build

> `build(capability)` · Stage 6 · Create
> **Prompt**: "Help me build the ability to ___."
> **Also heard as**: "Set this up for me." · "Make this something I can do every week." · "Automate ___."
> **Produces**: a new working capability, made by connecting parts and validated on real work.

## What it is for

A working capability directly changes what a person can do. Build turns a chosen practice into something the person's system can repeatedly accomplish: it connects models, tools, Skills, data, and the person's own steps, adds feedback that keeps the result strong, and proves the whole on a real task. OpenXSys turns understanding into new capability the person truly owns.

The engine underneath it is `../../../engines/build.md`, under “Creating capability.”

## What it produces

- **A working capability**: something that did a real task from the person's own work, with a result they accept.
- **Its parts and connections**, in the person's workspace: text first, then code, data and connections only as needed (see `system/parts.md`).
- **Its feedback**: how it checks its own output, and what happens when the check fails.
- **A record** another AI could run it from: what it does, how to start it, what it needs, what it was validated on, where it stops working.
- **A review date**, and a watch condition where one is useful.

## What a good use looks like

- The gap is named precisely before anything is built.
- The process was questioned and simplified first. **Automation comes last**: automating a step that should not exist makes the waste permanent.
- It is built first from what exists: the person's tools, Skills, material, and ways of working, together with capabilities already provided by the host. A new part earns its place when the existing combination still leaves the gulf open.
- Natural language comes first. A clear description that a capable AI can follow is often the whole capability. Code is added when a step must be exact, repeated or run without an AI present.
- A capability is more than a pipeline: it has feedback and a point of control. It knows when its output is poor, and the person can see and steer it.
- It handles the variety it will actually meet: the unusual input, the missing file, the bad day.
- It was **validated on real work**. Until it has done a real task acceptably, it is a design, and it is described as one.
- The person can inspect everything. Code is readable, runs with their knowledge, and says what it reads and writes.
- Rights are clear: spending, publishing, deletion, contact with others, recurring execution, and installation of new components follow real ownership and authority.
- It ends with one honest sentence on what now works, and the **Ability** signal only if it does.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Collecting tools.** Ten things installed, nothing that works end to end.
- **Automating a bad process.**
- **Never run on a real task.**
- **No feedback**, so quality drifts unseen.
- **Opaque code**, or anything the person cannot inspect.
- **Too much at once**, when a small working version would have taught more.
- **Claiming it works** before it has.

## It works with

Build follows **Best Practice** and **Design**, and is followed by **Review** (did it do what was intended?) and **Watch** (is it still working?). It sits in *Solve a recurring problem* and *Improve how I work*, and it is the "automate last", "standardize", "elevate", "do" and "act" step of the master routes. A capability that keeps working becomes an ability in the person's system (see `system/growth.md`).

## Teach-back

"This was Build: name exactly what you cannot do yet. Simplify the task before you automate any of it. Connect what you already have, add a check on the result, and run it on something real. It is yours when it has worked once on real work."

## The thinking behind it

- **Herbert A. Simon**, *The Sciences of the Artificial* (1969): complex capabilities are assembled from stable sub-assemblies.
- **Douglas Engelbart**, *Augmenting Human Intellect: A Conceptual Framework* (1962): a person's capability comes from the whole system of the human with their language, artifacts, methods and training, and is raised by improving that system together.
- **W. Ross Ashby**, *An Introduction to Cybernetics* (1956): a regulator needs at least as much variety as the disturbances it must handle.

OpenXSys simplifies: these describe how capability arises in general; Build applies them to one person's working setup with the tools at hand.
