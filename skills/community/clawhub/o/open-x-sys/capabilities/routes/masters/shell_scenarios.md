# Shell's scenarios

> **Master route.** Driving forces → key uncertainties → several futures → signals to watch.
> **Run it with**: "Run Shell-style scenarios on ___." · "What are the possible futures for ___, and how would I know which is coming?"
> **Fits**: a subject with a long horizon and deep uncertainty: an industry, a technology, a career, a region, a regulation. It is for decisions that have to hold up in more than one future.
> **Source**: Pierre Wack, "Scenarios: Uncharted Waters Ahead" and "Scenarios: Shooting the Rapids", *Harvard Business Review* (1985), describing scenario planning at Royal Dutch/Shell.

## The four steps, in their own order

| # | Step | What it asks | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Driving forces** | What forces are shaping this subject, and which outcomes are already settled by events that have already happened? | Treating everything as uncertain, or nothing |
| 2 | **Key uncertainties** | Of what is truly uncertain, which few things matter most? | Scenarios built on trivia |
| 3 | **Several futures** | What coherent, different worlds follow from different outcomes of those uncertainties? | One forecast with a high and a low version |
| 4 | **Signals to watch** | What early events would show which future is unfolding? | Scenarios read once and filed |

Wack separated **predetermined elements**, consequences of things that have already occurred and will play out whatever anyone does, from real **uncertainties**. Much of the value of the method lies in that separation.

## As OpenXSys methods

| Step | Method | How it is used here |
|---|---|---|
| Driving forces | **Flow** or **Loops** | What moves and accumulates in this system, what feeds back, and what is already in the pipeline |
| Key uncertainties, several futures | **What-if** | Follow the different outcomes of the key uncertainties through the structure, with delays and other actors' responses |
| Signals to watch | **Watch** | Record the early signals, their thresholds and what each would mean |

## What a good run looks like

- It establishes how the subject works before projecting it. Scenarios with no model underneath are stories.
- **Predetermined elements are stated first**, with evidence: demographic facts, capacity already under construction, contracts already signed, physical limits. These appear in every scenario.
- It selects two or three uncertainties that are both important and really open, and says why these.
- The futures are **few, distinct and internally consistent**: each is a world in which the parts fit together, described through cause and effect and not through adjectives. They are not labelled good, middle and bad.
- Each future is given a plain name that helps the person remember it.
- The run asks what the person's plans and assumptions look like in each future. The purpose is to change how the person sees their situation, which is more than to describe what may come.
- It identifies what holds in all futures (act on it) and what differs (stay flexible, or decide what to watch).
- Signals are observable and dated, and are recorded as watch conditions in the person's system.
- It assigns no probabilities it cannot support, and says why.

## Where OpenXSys simplifies

- At Shell, scenario work was done by a team over long periods, with extensive interviews and repeated presentation to managers, aimed at changing their shared assumptions. A single run gives a compact version. It can inform one person's thinking and cannot replace that process in an organization.
- Wack wrote about the practice as he led it in the early 1970s. Later scenario methods, at Shell and elsewhere, add steps this route does not use.
- The mapping of "driving forces" to **Flow** and **Loops** is OpenXSys's own.

## Next choices that usually fit

**Invert** on the person's current plan in the harshest scenario. **Weigh**, for a decision that must be made now across the scenarios. **Boyd's loop**, when one of the signals fires and the situation starts to move quickly.
