Confidence marks: **[A]** well established and widely reported · **[B]** reported by a named source, worth checking · **[C]** approximate or commonly cited without a firm source.

## What happened

- **[A]** On 26 April 1956 the *Ideal X*, a converted tanker owned by the trucking entrepreneur Malcom McLean, sailed from Newark, New Jersey to Houston, Texas carrying 58 containers on a specially built deck.
- **[B]** Marc Levinson reports that loading loose cargo onto a medium-sized ship cost about 5.83 US dollars per ton in 1956, and that loading the *Ideal X* cost about 15.8 cents per ton. (Levinson, *The Box*.)
- **[A]** McLean's company became Sea-Land Service. He came from trucking, not shipping.
- **[A]** The container itself was not new in 1956. Boxes for cargo had been tried by railways and shipping lines for decades.

## Before the container

- **[A]** General cargo moved as "break-bulk": sacks, barrels, crates and bales handled piece by piece by gangs of longshoremen.
- **[B]** Ships commonly spent as much time in port being loaded and unloaded as they did at sea.
- **[B]** Port handling was a large share of the total cost of moving goods by sea, often larger than the ocean voyage itself. Pilferage and damage were routine.
- **[A]** Factories and warehouses clustered near docks, in cities such as New York, London and Liverpool, because moving goods to and from the ship was expensive.

## What had to change besides the box

- **[A]** Ships: purpose-built cellular container ships replaced converted vessels during the 1960s.
- **[A]** Ports: container cranes, large paved yards and deep berths. Old finger piers in city centres could not be adapted.
- **[A]** Standards: after years of negotiation, international standards for container dimensions and corner fittings were adopted in the 1960s. The twenty-foot equivalent unit (TEU) became the unit of count.
- **[A]** Land transport: trucks, chassis and rail cars built for the same box, so that a container could move from factory to ship to train to customer without being opened.
- **[A]** Labour: dock work changed from large gangs to a small number of crane and equipment operators. On the US West Coast the 1960 Mechanization and Modernization Agreement between the longshore union and employers exchanged acceptance of new methods for guarantees to existing workers.
- **[B]** Regulation and contracts: freight rates, customs procedures and liability rules had been written for cargo handled piece by piece.
- **[B]** The US military's use of containers to supply forces in Vietnam from 1967 is often cited as a turning point in proving the system at scale.

## What followed

- **[A]** Old ports declined and new ones rose: the Manhattan and Brooklyn waterfronts lost traffic to Newark and Elizabeth in New Jersey; London's upstream docks closed while Felixstowe grew; Oakland overtook San Francisco.
- **[A]** Ship size grew by roughly two orders of magnitude: from a few hundred TEU in early vessels to more than 20,000 TEU in the largest ships of the 2020s.
- **[C]** A commonly cited figure is that about 90 percent of non-bulk cargo worldwide now moves in containers.
- **[B]** Bernhofen, El-Sahli and Kneller estimate that containerization had a large effect on trade among industrialized countries in the two decades after adoption, larger in their estimates than that of trade agreements.
- **[A]** Manufacturing no longer needed to be near a port or near its customers, and production of a single product could be divided among several countries.
- **[A]** In March 2021 the container ship *Ever Given* ran aground in the Suez Canal and blocked it for six days.

## Numbers worth sizing for yourself

A run can estimate these from first numbers and then check them:

- the labour hours to load a ship piece by piece against by container;
- the share of a product's shelf price that is ocean freight, for something small and valuable and for something bulky and cheap;
- how many containers a 20,000 TEU ship replaces in terms of 1956-sized ships.

## Points where accounts differ

- How much of the growth in world trade is due to the container, as against tariff reductions, rising incomes and other transport improvements.
- Whether McLean should be seen as the inventor, or as the person who put an existing idea into a complete system.
- Who gained and who lost: consumers, port cities, dock workers, exporters in different countries.

## Primary and standard sources

- Marc Levinson, *The Box: How the Shipping Container Made the World Smaller and the World Economy Bigger* (Princeton University Press, 2006; second edition 2016).
- Daniel Bernhofen, Zouheir El-Sahli and Richard Kneller, "Estimating the effects of the container revolution on world trade", *Journal of International Economics* (2016).
- International Organization for Standardization, standards on freight container dimensions and corner fittings (ISO 668 and related).
- United Nations Conference on Trade and Development, *Review of Maritime Transport* (annual), for current fleet and trade figures.
