# Materials: why wider roads fill up

[Chinese edition](https://github.com/gengshupeng/open-x-sys/blob/main/skills/open-x-sys.zh-CN/experience/playground/subjects/road-congestion.md)

> **A ready subject for the Playground: a city's traffic.** It is the default subject of the guided tour.
> **Status of these materials**: written on 2026-11-16 from published sources, from memory of those sources, and **not yet verified against the primary texts**. Each item carries a confidence mark. Verify figures before publishing anything based on them.
> **These are materials, not conclusions.** They contain findings and cases. What structure produces them, and what a city could do, are for the run to find.

Confidence marks: **[A]** well established and widely reported · **[B]** reported by a named source, worth checking · **[C]** approximate, disputed or commonly cited without a firm source.

## The common intuition

- Congestion means there is not enough road for the cars. Adding lanes adds capacity, so it should reduce congestion.

## Findings

- **[A]** Anthony Downs (1962) proposed that on urban commuter expressways, peak-hour traffic rises to meet maximum capacity. He later described "triple convergence": when a road gets faster, drivers switch to it from other routes, from other times of day and from other modes.
- **[A]** Gilles Duranton and Matthew Turner (2011) studied US metropolitan areas and found that vehicle-kilometres travelled on interstate highways rise in close to direct proportion to the lane-kilometres of those highways. They call this the fundamental law of road congestion.
- **[B]** In the same study, the added travel came mainly from more driving by existing residents, more commercial and truck traffic, and new residents; diversion from other roads was a small part. They also found that adding public transport service did not reduce vehicle-kilometres travelled.
- **[A]** Dietrich Braess (1968) showed mathematically that adding a link to a road network can increase everyone's travel time when each driver chooses their own fastest route.
- **[B]** Sally Cairns, Stephen Atkins and Phil Goodwin (2002) reviewed cases where road capacity was removed and reported that a substantial share of the traffic that had used it did not reappear on nearby roads.
- **[A]** William Vickrey argued from the 1950s and 1960s that road space is a scarce resource with no price at the point of use, and proposed charging for it according to congestion.

## Cases

- **[C]** The Katy Freeway (Interstate 10) in Houston was widened in a project completed around 2008 at a reported cost of about 2.8 billion dollars, to more than twenty lanes at its widest counting frontage and managed lanes. Analyses of travel-time data have reported that peak-period travel times on parts of the corridor were longer a few years later than before. The interpretation is disputed, since the region's population also grew.
- **[B]** In Seoul, the elevated Cheonggyecheon expressway was removed between 2003 and 2005 and the stream beneath it restored. Reports afterwards describe traffic adjusting without the predicted gridlock.
- **[B]** London introduced a daily congestion charge for the central zone on 17 February 2003. Transport for London reported that traffic entering the zone fell by roughly 15 to 20 percent in the first years.
- **[B]** Stockholm ran a congestion-charge trial in 2006 and made it permanent in 2007 after a referendum. Studies report traffic across the cordon about 20 percent lower, and that public support rose after people experienced it.
- **[A]** Singapore has charged for road use in its central area since 1975 and electronically since 1998.
- **[C]** New York began charging vehicles entering lower Manhattan in January 2025. **Check current results before use.**

## Numbers worth sizing for yourself

- If a lane carries about 2,000 vehicles an hour, how many new car trips does one added lane accommodate in a three-hour peak, and how does that compare with the number of people in the region who currently avoid the road at that time?
- How long after a widening would you expect travel times to return to their earlier level, given how fast people change routes, times and where they live?
- What is a minute of delay worth, summed over everyone on the road?

## Points where accounts differ

- Whether added travel is a cost or a benefit: the new trips are trips people wanted to make.
- How far the US findings apply to cities with different densities and transport systems.
- Whether pricing is fair, and to whom.
- What happens over decades, as land use changes around a road.
