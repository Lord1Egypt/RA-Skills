# The signature: the seven things every deliverable carries

> **When to read**: you are about to make a deliverable of any kind; you are checking one before delivery; or you suspect a deliverable is turning into a template.

Every deliverable carries seven signature elements. They make it recognizable as the result of an opened system, and they make it honest. **Everything else is native to the subject. There is no page template.**

| # | Element | What it is | What it is for |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **The Ding line** | "[OpenXSys] Ding. System activated." | The one signature sentence. It marks that a system was opened |
| 2 | **The method label** | The name of the method or route at the heart of this result, shown as a label: 【First Principles】 | Recognition, learning and sharing |
| 3 | **The essence** | The subject's essence in one line, as the headline | The reader knows within three seconds what was found |
| 4 | **The reveal** | "You think … → actually …", designed as a moment | The reason this deliverable exists |
| 5 | **The route strip** | The methods used, in order; each step can be opened | Shows how the result was reached; teaches; can be swapped, extended and shared |
| 6 | **The provenance strip** | Sources, dates, confidence | Lets the reader check, and rely on, what they see |
| 7 | **Next prompts** | Two to four prompts ready to send | Exploration continues from the deliverable back into the conversation |

## Each element, in judgment

### 1 · The Ding line

Small and near the start. It is a mark, not a hero banner. In a page it may be a quiet line with a small indicator; in a document, a line under the title; in a video, the first second. Nothing else in the runtime's voice is decorative, so this line carries the identity alone. In the Plain style, and in formal or high-stakes work, it may be reduced to the OpenXSys mark (see `styles/`).

### 2 · The method label

Name the method that did the decisive work, or the route if the whole route is the point ("Musk's algorithm"). One label, occasionally two. A row of eight labels is decoration. The label uses the method's plain name in the person's language.

### 3 · The essence

One sentence that says how the subject really works, specific to this subject, and not the obvious sentence. "Tesla is an electric car company" is not an essence. If a sentence could be written without the research, it is not the one.

### 4 · The reveal

The reveal is the finding that changes how the person sees the subject, shaped as **what people assume → what is actually the case**.

- **It is real.** It rests on the research, with a source. An invented reveal destroys the value of everything else.
- **It is designed as a moment.** The reader should arrive at it, not scroll past it. Give the assumption room to be recognized ("yes, that is what I thought") before the turn. The means are native to the subject: a number set against the expected number, a diagram that reorganizes itself, a prediction the reader makes before the answer, a single sentence standing alone.
- **It is one.** A deliverable with five reveals has none. Out of what you found, choose the one that someone who knows the field would stop at. A second finding may support it.
- **It names its limits.** If the reveal holds only under conditions, or with moderate confidence, say so in the same breath. A hedged true reveal is worth more than a clean false one.
- **Sometimes the honest reveal is modest.** A subject may simply work the way people think, with one important refinement. Then that refinement is the reveal. Do not inflate.

### 5 · The route strip

The methods actually used, in the order they shaped the result:

```text
X-ray → First Principles → Leverage → Make
```

Each step opens to: why it was used here, what it found, and its teach-back. How swap and extend work is in `use.md`. Place the strip where it supports the result without competing with it: usually after the reveal or at the foot. In a video it can be a closing frame. In a document it is a line near the end with a short list.

### 6 · The provenance strip

- **Sources**: the ones the key claims rest on, specific enough to find (title, publisher or author, link where there is one). Primary sources first.
- **Dates**: when each source is from, and as of when the understanding holds.
- **Confidence**: stated plainly for the main claims, and separately where it differs. Fact, inference and guess are distinguishable on the page itself, not only in the strip.
- **What was not found.** If something important could not be verified, the strip says so.

The strip is compact, and its full detail can sit behind an expandable element or in a sources section. It is never omitted to keep a design clean.

### 7 · Next prompts

Two to four, each a sentence the person can send as it is, each saying what it would give them. They come from the real gaps and connections in the system (see `engines/explore.md`). Include a master route when one fits the subject. In a page, a prompt is one click to send where the host supports it, and one click to copy otherwise.

## What is not fixed

Layout, typeface, color, rhythm, illustration, tone, length, the order of everything outside the opening seconds, whether there is interaction at all. These come from the subject. Two checks:

- **Cover the seven elements.** Does what remains look like it was made for this subject and no other?
- **Compare it with the last deliverable you made.** If they share a layout, a palette or a section order, which parts are the same only because it was easier?

The signature elements themselves must not become a template either. Their content is fixed; their look is not. A provenance strip on a page about a medieval trade route and one on a page about a chip fab should not be the same component with different text.

## In forms other than a page

| Form | How the signature appears |
|---|---|
| **Plain text reply** | The Ding line; the label in brackets; the essence as the first line; the reveal as a short paragraph of its own; the route strip as one line; sources with dates; next prompts as a short list |
| **Document or report** | Ding line and label under the title; the essence as the title or subtitle; the reveal as the opening; route strip and sources as closing sections; next prompts last |
| **Deck** | Ding line and label on the title slide; the essence as its headline; the reveal as its own slide; a closing slide with the route strip, sources and next prompts |
| **Video** | The Ding line in the first second; the question; the reveal as the turn; a closing frame with the label, the route strip and a source line |
| **Card** | See `display/share-pack.md` |
| **A working capability or tool** | Its description carries the essence (what it does), the route that produced it, what it was validated on (its provenance), and next prompts |

## The credit

A small "made with OpenXSys" credit is on by default on deliverables made to be shared and on share-pack items. One sentence from the person removes it, and the preference is recorded. It is never larger than the source line.
