# Musk's algorithm

> **Master route.** Question every requirement → delete → simplify → accelerate → automate last.
> **Run it with**: "Run Musk's algorithm on ___." · "用马斯克五步法过一遍 ___。"
> **Fits**: a process, a product, a workflow, a routine, a set of rules: anything that has accumulated steps and requirements over time.
> **Source**: Walter Isaacson, *Elon Musk* (2023), which describes the five steps Musk calls "the algorithm".

## The five steps, in their own order

The order is the method. Each step is a mistake if done before the ones above it.

| # | Step | What it asks | The mistake it prevents |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | **Question every requirement** | Who asked for this, by name, and why? Is it still true? Requirements from clever people deserve the most scrutiny, because they are the least likely to be questioned | Carefully building what nobody needs |
| 2 | **Delete** any part or step you can | What happens if this is simply removed? If almost nothing ever has to be put back, not enough was deleted | Keeping things "in case" |
| 3 | **Simplify and optimize** | What is the simplest form of what remains? | Optimizing something that should not exist |
| 4 | **Accelerate** cycle time | How can what remains go faster? | Speeding up a process that is still wrong |
| 5 | **Automate** | Which of what remains should run by itself? | Automating waste, which makes it permanent |

## As OpenXSys methods

| Step | Method | How it is used here |
|---|---|---|
| Question every requirement | **First Principles** | Separate what must be true from what is habit or assumption. For each requirement, find its owner and its reason; where the person's material does not say, mark it as unknown and ask |
| Delete, simplify | **Best Practice** | Decide what to remove and what the simplest sufficient form is, for this system's real conditions. Uses **Vital Few** to see which parts carry the result |
| Accelerate | **Flow**, **Bottleneck** | Find what sets the cycle time now, and shorten that |
| Automate last | **Build** | Turn what survived into a working capability, validated on real work |
| Then | **Review** | Compare with the starting point: what was removed, what had to come back, what changed |

## What a good run looks like

- The subject is described first as it actually is: its steps, who does them, how long each takes. If the person gave only a short description, the run works from that, says what it assumed, and marks what needs checking against reality.
- All five steps appear, **in order**, each applied to what the previous step left. The page makes visible that step 3 is working on a smaller thing than step 1 saw.
- Step 1 names requirements one by one, each with its owner and reason, or "owner unknown".
- Step 2 is bold. It proposes real deletions and says for each what could go wrong and how it would be noticed. It expects that some will have to be restored and says so.
- Step 5 comes last, and is often small. A run that ends with "automate everything" has skipped steps 1 to 4.
- The result is a before and after the person can act on: the process as it was, the process proposed, what to try first, and what to watch.
- Deleting steps from a real process is the person's decision. The run recommends and prepares; it does not remove anything from their files, tools or team routines without being asked.

## Where OpenXSys simplifies

- Isaacson reports the algorithm as rules Musk repeats in the context of manufacturing at Tesla and SpaceX. OpenXSys applies it to any process, including one person's routine, where "requirements" may be habits and "parts" may be meetings.
- The original includes personal accountability for each requirement inside an organization. An AI cannot assign accountability; it can ask who owns a requirement and record the answer.
- **Review** is an OpenXSys addition. The original list ends at automation.
- The steps are paraphrased here, not quoted. Before the wording of any step is presented as Musk's own, it is checked against the book.

## Next choices that usually fit

## A note on its own subject

This route is often run on Musk's own companies, as in OpenXSys's launch demonstration. The same honesty applies: what the companies actually did is established from sources, with dates, and is kept apart from what the algorithm would recommend.
