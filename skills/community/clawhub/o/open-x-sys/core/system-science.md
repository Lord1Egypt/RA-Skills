# How Systems Science Changes a Judgment

Use these principles to find the part of a real object that best explains change. One mechanism that can be stated, checked, and used to change a judgment carries more weight than a display of many concepts. The principles below may be selected and combined, or replaced by domain knowledge that explains the current object better.

## Hierarchy, boundary, and emergence

A boundary serves the question being asked. For one product, a factory may remain external supply. If every delay arrives through the supply boundary, place factory, purchasing, and inventory at the same scale. Interactions that are nearly independent over a short period may be understood separately. As time expands, coupling strengthens, or a threshold is crossed, look again at how they form a whole. Local mechanisms may amplify or cancel one another at the whole-system level, or create behavior no part has alone. [Herbert Simon, “The Architecture of Complexity”](https://www.andrew.cmu.edu/course/15-440/assets/READINGS/simon-architecture-of-complexity-1962.pdf)

Before changing scale, ask whether two conclusions are actually about the same thing. A machine can be faster while the project becomes slower. The first may measure minutes per operation; the second may measure total waiting from request to delivery. Find how much time was saved locally, where the burden moved, and which coupling has enough evidence to support a cross-scale explanation.

## Stocks, flows, delays, and feedback

First distinguish **what has accumulated**, **how much enters and leaves per unit time**, and **how those rates are regulated**. Inventory is a stock; arrivals and deliveries are flows. A waiting queue is also a stock. Speed and throughput remain distinct. Follow a causal chain through real information, matter, or behavior, then determine whether it returns to its origin. A loop closes only when a result changes the force that later causes it. Reinforcing and balancing loops can coexist and dominate at different times.

Delay makes present decisions depend on past reality. Two parts can each follow their rules and still fail repeatedly because their clocks miss each other. Compare those clocks: who responds in minutes, who decides by week, and who takes years to change. When useful, build a recalculable timeline or quantitative relation and state its observation window. Delay is part of the explanation and can be challenged by the actual sequence of events. [Donella Meadows, “Leverage Points”](https://donellameadows.org/archives/leverage-points-places-to-intervene-in-a-system/)

## Constraints, adaptation, and leverage

Find the condition that actually determines the whole-system result. Some constraints are physical; others come from rules, authority, information, or resource allocation. Improving one part may move the constraint. When repeated local repairs fail, ask whether the party that has the information, the party that can decide, and the party that sees the result are the same. Compare changes to flows, feedback, collaboration boundaries, and goals. Ask who bears the cost, who has the right to change, and how long the condition takes to respond.

A leverage point is an evidence-backed judgment. To test it, compare the same arrangement under different conditions or state which observable should move first after a change. If the user only wants to understand the object, a testable explanation made into a work is already complete.

## Make explanations distinguishable and falsifiable

- **Which facts change the reader's mind.** Look for a paradox, counterexample, threshold, or unexpected flow that can carry the main line. Prefer primary material that changes the judgment. Make bold explanations and speak at the strength of the evidence.
- **How two explanations separate.** If both the first observer of a problem and the later fixer have plausible accounts, ask which timing, measurement, or counterfactual distinguishes them. Simulation compares consequences; observation of reality confirms which condition actually occurred.
- **How far the explanation holds.** Give a critical inference a time range, scale, and boundary conditions. When new data challenges it, revise the understanding instead of preserving an old conclusion for continuity.

## An example

Suppose a production team adopts a faster generation tool. Draft output doubles, yet publication slows. Several explanations are possible: review throughput cannot keep up and the queue accumulates; rejection rises and rework consumes capacity; or notifications are instant while approval remains concentrated in one person who checks once a week.

Draft arrival and review departure test the backlog explanation. Rework loops across comparable pieces test quality. The distribution of waiting against approval times tests authority and timing. Several mechanisms may coexist; evidence that distinguishes them identifies the dominant one. A deliverable conclusion can be as concrete as why “faster” made the team later, which relation makes it recur, and which observation would change the explanation.
