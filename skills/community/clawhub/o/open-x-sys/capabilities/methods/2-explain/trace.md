# Trace

> `trace(x)` · Stage 2 · Explain
> **Prompt**: "How did ___ become what it is today?"
> **Also heard as**: "Why is ___ built like this?" · "Where did ___ come from?" · "Was it always this way?"
> **Reveals**: turning points, and what got locked in.

## What it is for

Many features of a system make no sense as designs and perfect sense as histories. Trace follows how the subject became what it is: the few moments where it could have gone another way, what was chosen, and what became fixed afterwards so that the choice could no longer be undone. It separates what is there by necessity from what is there by accident and then stayed.

## What it reveals

- **Turning points**: the handful of moments that decided the present shape.
- **The alternatives** that were open at each, and why this one won.
- **What got locked in**, and by what: increasing returns, switching costs, standards, institutions, habit.
- **Three tempos**: fast events, medium-term cycles, and slow structures that barely move and constrain everything above them.
- **What is still open**: which past choices could be revisited, and at what cost.

## What a good use looks like

- It selects. Five turning points that explain the present are worth more than fifty dated events.
- At each turning point it reconstructs the situation as it looked then: what people knew, what else was possible. Hindsight makes outcomes look inevitable; they rarely were.
- It names the lock-in mechanism specifically. "It became the standard" is a result; the mechanism is why switching became costly.
- It separates accident from necessity by asking the counterfactual: would a different early choice have led somewhere else, or would deeper forces have produced much the same result?
- It looks under the events for the slow layer: geography, demography, technology base, law, long habits.
- Dates and sources are specific.

## Where it goes wrong

- **A chronology.** A list of dates explains nothing.
- **Inevitability.** Telling the story as if it could only have ended this way.
- **One hero.** Attributing a structure to one person's decisions and ignoring the conditions that made them possible.
- **Only events**, with the slow structure beneath them left out.
- **History for its own sake**, never connected back to what the subject is today.

## It works with

Trace follows **X-ray** in *Explain a surprise* and deepens **First Principles** in *Learn something deeply* (a convention's reason is usually found in its history). It feeds **What-if** (what is locked in limits the futures) and **Leverage** (what is still open is where change is possible). It is the "go back to the source" step of Feynman's technique.

## Teach-back

"This was Trace: do not list what happened. Find the few moments where it could have gone another way, ask what was chosen and why, and ask what became fixed afterwards. What is fixed explains the present; what is not fixed is where it can still change."

## The thinking behind it

- **W. Brian Arthur**, "Competing Technologies, Increasing Returns, and Lock-In by Historical Events" (1989) and *Increasing Returns and Path Dependence in the Economy* (1994): small early events can lock an economy into one outcome among several possible ones.
- **Fernand Braudel**, on the *longue durée* (1958): events ride on slower conjunctures and on very slow structures; history has several tempos.

OpenXSys simplifies: Arthur's argument is a formal economic one and Braudel's a programme for historians; Trace takes from them two questions, what got locked in and what moves slowly underneath.
