# Boundary

> `bound(x)` · Advanced lens
> **Prompt**: "Who and what are we leaving out of ___?"
> **Use it when**: an analysis feels complete and still misses; a plan affects people who had no say; costs keep appearing "from outside"; two people disagree and are in fact talking about different systems.

## What it shows

Every description of a system draws a line: this is inside, that is outside. The line is a choice, and it decides what counts as relevant, whose purposes are served, and what will be treated as someone else's problem. Boundary makes the choice visible and asks what moving the line would change.

It asks, of the system as it is being described:

- **Whose purposes** does it serve, and whose should it?
- **Who decides**, and what do they control?
- **What knowledge** is counted as relevant, and whose is ignored?
- **Who is affected but not involved**, and who speaks for them?
- **What is treated as outside**: costs, people, time horizons, neighbouring systems?

## What a good use looks like

- It names the current boundary explicitly, including the time horizon. Much of what is "outside" is simply later.
- It finds what crosses the boundary uncounted: costs pushed onto others, dependencies taken for granted, effects on people who were never asked.
- It tries at least one other boundary (wider, narrower, drawn around a different purpose) and shows what changes in the picture and in the recommendation.
- It distinguishes what is the case from what ought to be: who the system serves now, and who it should.
- It does not expand without limit. A wider boundary is better only when it changes the answer. The useful boundary is the smallest one that contains what decides the outcome.

## Where it goes wrong

- Widening until the system is "everything" and nothing can be said.
- Listing stakeholders without asking what each one changes.
- Treating the first boundary as a fact given by the world.

## It works with

Boundary refines **Open**, complements **Shift** (Shift takes positions inside the picture; Boundary asks who is missing from it), and tests **Connect** and **Weigh** (does the decision look the same when those left out are counted?).

## Teach-back

"This was Boundary: notice where you drew the line around the problem, including in time. Ask who and what fell outside it. Then draw it somewhere else once and see whether your answer survives."

## The thinking behind it

- **C. West Churchman**, *The Systems Approach* (1968): what is taken as the system and what as its environment is a judgment, and improvement depends on how widely it is drawn.
- **Werner Ulrich**, *Critical Heuristics of Social Planning* (1983): a set of boundary questions about sources of motivation, control, knowledge and legitimacy, asked in both "is" and "ought" form.

OpenXSys simplifies: Ulrich gives twelve boundary questions; this lens uses their four groups.
