# Connect

> `connect(a, b)` · Stage 3 · Anticipate
> **Prompt**: "How does ___ affect ___?"
> **Also heard as**: "What does ___ have to do with ___?" · "Who else does this hit?" · "Is this the same thing as ___?"
> **Reveals**: real relations between systems, and the ripples.

## What it is for

No system stands alone, and many surprises come from outside the boundary that was drawn. Connect examines the relation between two systems: what actually passes between them, in which direction, how strongly and how fast, and how a change in one travels into the other and beyond. It is also the method for bringing knowledge from one field to bear on another, and for seeing how the person's own systems relate.

## What it reveals

- **What crosses** between the two: money, people, material, information, demand, risk, attention.
- **Direction, strength and delay** of each crossing.
- **Shared resources and shared constraints**: things both depend on, through which they affect each other without touching.
- **Ripple paths**: A changes B, B changes C.
- **Common structure**: where the two work by the same mechanism, even with no contact between them.

## What a good use looks like

- Each relation is a mechanism: what moves, from where to where, and through what. A correlation or a shared headline is not a relation.
- It keeps two kinds of connection apart. **Coupling**: something really passes between the systems. **Same structure**: they work alike, so understanding one helps with the other. Both are useful, and they must never be confused.
- Strength and speed are stated, at least roughly. A weak, slow connection and a strong, fast one are different facts.
- It follows the ripple more than one step and says where it dies out.
- For a claimed common structure it shows the correspondence part by part, says how it could be checked, and says where it stops holding.
- For the person's own systems, it states **what the connection makes possible**: which understanding transfers, which tool or dataset can be reused, which deliverable can now be made. Then it uses it.
- It draws on more than one field's models to look at the same relation, and notes where they agree.

## Where it goes wrong

- **"Everything is connected."** True and useless.
- **Analogy presented as causation.**
- **Arrows without direction or strength.**
- **One step only**, missing where the effect actually lands.
- **A forced connection**, made because two things were mentioned together.

## It works with

Connect extends **Open** across the boundary and supports **What-if** and **And Then What?** (ripples into other systems). It is the "use many models" step of **Munger's checklist**. In the person's system it produces the connections recorded by `engines/evolve.md`. The **Boundary** lens asks whether the boundary itself should move.

## Teach-back

"This was Connect: between two things, ask what actually passes from one to the other, which way, how much and how fast. Keep 'they affect each other' separate from 'they work the same way'. Both are worth knowing; they are not the same claim."

## The thinking behind it

- **Ludwig von Bertalanffy**, *General System Theory* (1968): open systems exchange with their environment, and the same structural laws appear in different fields.
- **Herbert A. Simon**, "The Architecture of Complexity" (1962): complex systems are nearly decomposable; interactions between subsystems are weaker than those within, and still not negligible.
- **Charlie Munger**: using a range of models from many disciplines on one problem.
- **Qian Xuesen** and colleagues (1990): open complex giant systems exchange matter, energy and information with their surroundings.

OpenXSys simplifies: it turns these general positions into one practical distinction, coupling against common structure.
