# Tragedy of the commons

> Each user gains from using a shared, limited resource, while the cost of overuse is spread over everyone. Total use rises until the resource degrades. It is a real pattern and not an inevitable one.

## The structure

Several users draw on one shared resource of limited capacity. Each user's gain from using more goes to that user. The cost of the added use is shared by all, and delayed. So each has reason to use more, total use exceeds what the resource can bear, and the resource declines for everyone. There is no feedback from the total to the individual in time to matter.

## How to recognize it

- A **shared resource with limited capacity**: a fishery, a pasture, groundwater, the atmosphere, a road, a shared budget, a team's attention, a shared calendar, a common codebase.
- **Private gain, shared cost.**
- No individual's use is large enough to matter alone.
- Total use is rising and the resource's yield per unit of effort is falling.
- Everyone can see the problem, and no one can solve it alone.

## Where it usually breaks: it is not inevitable

Hardin's original argument assumed open access and users who do not communicate. Elinor Ostrom's studies of real communities showed that many **do govern shared resources successfully over long periods**, without either privatizing them or handing them to a central authority. Arrangements that last tend to have:

- clear boundaries: who may use the resource, and what the resource is;
- rules that fit local conditions;
- users who take part in changing the rules;
- monitoring, by the users or by people answerable to them;
- sanctions that start mild and increase;
- cheap, quick ways to resolve conflicts;
- recognition by outside authorities of the users' right to organize;
- for large resources, governance organized in several nested layers.

So the pattern breaks, in the good sense, when users can see total use, talk to one another, make and enforce their own rules, and trust that others are bound too. It persists when access is open, users are anonymous, and feedback is slow.

## Where to push

Make total use and the state of the resource visible to every user. Create a place where the users can agree on rules. Let those who use it monitor it. Connect each user's use to its cost. Apply a boundary. Choose private ownership or outside regulation only after asking whether the users could govern it themselves.

## What to check before naming it

Identify the resource, its limit and its users. Show private gain against shared cost. Then ask whether a governance arrangement already exists, because many apparent commons are in fact governed.

## Sources

- **Garrett Hardin**, "The Tragedy of the Commons", *Science* (1968).
- **Elinor Ostrom**, *Governing the Commons* (1990): evidence from long-enduring institutions for common-pool resources, and the design principles that characterize them.
