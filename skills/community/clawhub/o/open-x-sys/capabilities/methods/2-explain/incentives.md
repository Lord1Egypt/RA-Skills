# Incentives

> `incentives(x)` · Stage 2 · Explain
> **Prompt**: "Who gains what from ___ staying the way it is?"
> **Also heard as**: "Why does nobody fix ___?" · "Why would they do that?" · "Who benefits?"
> **Reveals**: who wants what, who pays, and why behavior follows.

## What it is for

When a system keeps producing an outcome that nobody says they want, the usual explanation is that the people in it are responding sensibly to what they are rewarded and punished for. Incentives maps those rewards. It explains behavior without needing anyone to be foolish or dishonest, and it shows why appeals to do better fail while the rewards stay the same.

## What it reveals

- **The actors** whose behavior shapes the outcome.
- **What each gains and loses**: money, time, status, safety, effort, blame.
- **How each is measured and paid**, and over what time horizon.
- **Who pays the cost**, and whether that is the one who decides.
- **The gap** between what the system says it wants and what it actually rewards.

## What a good use looks like

- Each actor's behavior is explained from their own position: given what they are measured on and what happens to them when things go wrong, what would a reasonable person do?
- It includes the non-monetary: avoiding blame, saving effort, keeping status and keeping one's job often outweigh money.
- It finds where the one who decides is not the one who pays. Those separations explain a great deal.
- It compares the stated goal with the rewarded behavior, with evidence: the actual pay scheme, the actual metric, the actual promotion criteria, not their descriptions.
- It notes the time horizon. A reward this quarter against a cost in five years predicts the choice.
- It says what change in rewards would change the behavior, and what that change would cost and whom it would upset.
- Claims about real people and organizations rest on sources. Motives are inferred from structure and labelled as inference.

## Where it goes wrong

- **Cynicism.** Reducing everything to self-interest. People also act from norms, identity, loyalty and habit, and an account that ignores this predicts badly.
- **Accusation.** Asserting that named people act in bad faith, without evidence. The method explains behavior through structure; it does not judge character.
- **Only money.**
- **Taking the stated incentive for the real one.**
- **Stopping at "follow the money"**, with no account of what would change the behavior.

## It works with

Incentives follows **X-ray** in *Explain a surprise* and pairs with **Shift** (what each position sees) and **Loops** (incentives are often the links of a loop). It feeds **Leverage**: changing what is rewarded is one of the stronger places to push. It is the second step of **Munger's checklist**.

## Teach-back

"This was Incentives: when people keep doing something that seems wrong, ask what they are rewarded for, what they are punished for, and who bears the cost. Usually they are being reasonable inside a structure that is not."

## The thinking behind it

- **Charlie Munger**, "The Psychology of Human Misjudgment" (talk, 1995; later revised): the power of incentives is placed first among the tendencies that shape behavior, and he argues it is routinely underestimated.

OpenXSys simplifies: Munger's talk lists many psychological tendencies; this method takes the first and joins it to a structural question, who decides and who pays.
