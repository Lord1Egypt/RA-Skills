# Compounding

> A reinforcing pattern. Growth in proportion to what has already accumulated, so that the gains themselves produce gains.

## The structure

A stock grows at a rate proportional to its own size: money earning a return that is reinvested, knowledge that makes new knowledge easier to gain, trust that opens doors that build more trust, an audience that brings more audience. The result is exponential: a long, flat-looking stretch followed by a steep rise.

## How to recognize it

- The gains are **added back to the base**, and the base is what produces the next gains.
- The rate is roughly stable over a long period.
- Small differences in rate, or a few more years, produce very large differences in outcome.
- It looks unimpressive for a long time. People who expect steady, linear progress conclude that it is not working.

## Where it usually breaks

- **Interruption.** Compounding depends on not stopping. Withdrawals, long pauses or starting over reset the base.
- **Large losses.** A loss of half requires a gain of one hundred percent to recover. Avoiding ruin matters more than raising the rate.
- **The rate falls as the base grows.** What compounds at a small size often cannot at a large one. See [limits to growth](limits_to_growth.md).
- **Quitting before the curve bends**, because early results look small.
- **It works on bad things too.** Debt, technical debt, bad habits and distrust compound by the same arithmetic.
- **Claimed where gains are not reinvested.** If each period's result is consumed and the base does not grow, the growth is linear at best.

## Where to push

Start early. Protect the base from large losses. Keep the gains in. Prefer a durable moderate rate to a high fragile one. Stop a negative compounding process early, while it is small.

## What to check before naming it

Identify the stock, the rate and the mechanism by which gains return to the stock. Ask what would interrupt it and what caps the rate.

## Source

Compound growth is elementary mathematics and has no single author. It is the simplest reinforcing loop in system dynamics (see `methods/1-see/loops.md` and Forrester, *Industrial Dynamics*, 1961).
