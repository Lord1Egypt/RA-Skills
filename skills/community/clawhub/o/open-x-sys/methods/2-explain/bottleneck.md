# Bottleneck

> `bottleneck(x)` · Stage 2 · Explain
> **Prompt**: "What really limits ___?"
> **Also heard as**: "Why can't we go faster?" · "What is holding ___ back?" · "Where is ___ stuck?"
> **Reveals**: the one constraint that limits the whole.

## What it is for

At any moment a system's output is limited by very few things, usually one. Improving anything else changes nothing, however much effort it takes. Bottleneck finds that one constraint. It explains why hard work in many places has produced little, and it tells the person where an hour of effort is worth more than a hundred hours elsewhere.

## What it reveals

- **The goal** the system is limited in reaching. A constraint only exists relative to a goal.
- **The constraint**: the step, resource, rule or belief that sets the pace of the whole.
- **The evidence**: where work waits, what is always busy, what everything else depends on.
- **Its kind**: physical (a machine, a person, a budget), a policy (a rule, a sign-off, a measure), or outside the system (demand, a supplier, a regulation).
- **Where it will move** once it is relieved.

## What a good use looks like

- The goal is stated first, in terms that can be measured.
- It names **one** constraint and shows why it, and not the others, sets the pace. If two candidates remain, it says what observation would decide between them.
- The evidence is concrete: the queue in front of it, its utilization, the waiting time, what happens to the whole when it stops for an hour.
- It distinguishes the pile from its cause. Work piles up before the constraint; the pile is the symptom.
- It checks for policy constraints. In knowledge work the limit is rarely a machine and often a rule, an approval, a habit or the way people are measured.
- It says what the non-constraints should do meanwhile: work to the pace of the constraint, not to their own maximum.
- It predicts where the constraint goes next (see `patterns/moving_bottleneck.md`).

## Where it goes wrong

- **Ten bottlenecks.** A list of everything that is slow is not a finding.
- **Local efficiency.** Making a non-constraint faster only builds a bigger pile.
- **No goal**, so anything can be called a limit.
- **Assuming the limit is a resource** ("we need more people") without checking whether a rule is the limit.
- **Naming the constraint and stopping**, with no word on how to use it better before spending on it.

## It works with

Bottleneck follows **Flow** (the pile-up points to it) and **Loops**, and leads to **Leverage** and **Best Practice**. It sits in *Solve a recurring problem* and *Improve how I work*, is the first of **Goldratt's five focusing steps**, and is a step of the Toyota way. The **Contradiction** lens helps when relieving the constraint conflicts with something else that is needed.

## Teach-back

"This was Bottleneck: say what the system is for, then find the one thing that sets its pace. Look for where work waits. Everything else can run faster and it will not matter."

## The thinking behind it

- **Eliyahu Goldratt**, *The Goal* (1984): every system has a constraint that limits its throughput, and improvement elsewhere is an illusion. Goldratt later stated the five focusing steps explicitly: identify the constraint, exploit it, subordinate everything else to it, elevate it, and repeat.

OpenXSys simplifies: Goldratt wrote about manufacturing plants and their measures; the method is used here for any system with a goal, including one person's work.
